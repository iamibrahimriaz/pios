# Evaluation

---

# Overview

One test runs through all of these: **could the comparison have produced a different
winner?** If not, it was a record rather than a decision.

---

# Exercise 1 — Generate an Option You Would Hate to Pick

For any product you are working on, write a third option that involves not building the
software: a service, a partnership, a manual process, an integration into something that
already exists.

**Passing looks like.** The option is genuinely viable and you can argue for it.

**Failing looks like.** It is obviously worse. Then it is padding, and the comparison
still has one real candidate.

---

# Exercise 2 — Weights First

Take a decision you have already made. Write the criteria and their weights, with a
justification for each weight, *without* looking at the options.

**Passing looks like.** At least one weight is justified by an upstream finding — a
declared shortfall, a switching cost, a regulatory constraint, a timing verdict.

**Failing looks like.** Every weight is a round number with no stated reason. Those are
preferences with arithmetic around them.

---

# Exercise 3 — Score Blind, Then Total

Score three options on each criterion separately, and do not compute the totals until
every cell is filled.

**Passing looks like.** You are uncertain of the winner while scoring.

**Failing looks like.** You knew from the second row. That is the laundering signal, and
it is worth noticing rather than suppressing.

---

# Exercise 4 — Cut Something That Hurts

Write three non-goals for a product you care about, each one something you actively want
to build.

**Passing looks like.** Writing them is unpleasant.

**Failing looks like.** They are things nobody proposed. Safe non-goals are the most
common form and they hold nothing.

---

# Exercise 5 — State the MVP's Question

Write, in one sentence, what your MVP is designed to find out. Then check whether the
smallest version that answers it is what you planned to build.

**Passing looks like.** The smallest version is noticeably smaller than the plan — and
sometimes is not software.

**Failing looks like.** No single question. Then the MVP is a small product, and it will
launch without generating any information.

---

# Exercise 6 — Trace a Shortfall

If module 04 declared a shortfall, point at the specific weight in your comparison that
changed because of it, and check whether the winner would differ without it.

**Passing looks like.** You can point at it, and the winner does differ.

**Failing looks like.** Nothing changed. Then the shortfall was documented rather than
carried — which is the framework's most damaging failure and the hardest to see.

---

# Exercise 7 — Assign Every Risk

Take a risk register and put a person's name on every mitigation.

**Passing looks like.** Some names repeat uncomfortably, revealing that one person owns
more than they can carry. That is a finding.

**Failing looks like.** You cannot name anyone for several. Those mitigations will not
happen, and the register is documenting foreknowledge of a problem nobody will address.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Produces a chosen approach with reasons written afterward |
| **Working** | Generates real options; sets weights during scoring |
| **Competent** | Weights justified upstream and set first; non-goals that hurt; MVP framed as a question |
| **Fluent** | Has been surprised by their own comparison, and can point at the weight a declared shortfall moved |

The move to fluent is marked by a single experience: running the comparison honestly and
losing to an option you did not want.

---

# A Note on What Cannot Be Assessed Here

Whether the choice was right. Nothing here tests that and nothing can — the information
that would settle it does not exist yet. What these exercises test is whether the choice
was *made*, which is a different property and the only one available in advance.

A well-made choice can be wrong. A laundered choice can be right. The framework
optimizes for the first, because only the first can be corrected when the evidence
arrives.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> The question is not whether you picked well. It is whether you could have picked
> otherwise.
>
> A comparison that could not have gone the other way did not compare anything.
