# Weaknesses

---

# What It Is

Where each competitor falls short — sorted by whether they **could** fix it, not by how visible it is.

| Kind of weakness | Can they fix it? | Worth building on? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Commercial conflict** — fixing it undercuts their revenue | No, rationally | Yes — the strongest available |
| **Customer obligation** — their largest accounts would object | Rarely | Yes |
| **Wrong sales motion** — they cannot sell to this segment profitably | Slowly and expensively | Yes |
| **Architectural** — it requires rebuilding something central | Eventually, at cost | Sometimes, with a duration attached |
| **Segment neglect** — this segment is too small to interest them | Only if it grows | Yes, until it grows |
| Missing feature | Yes, in a quarter | No |
| Poor interface | Yes | No |

The bottom two rows are the ones a comparison exercise surfaces first, and they are the ones that close fastest.

---

# When It Applies

Alongside Move 3 (Test) and decisively in Move 5 (Locate) — a weakness is only an opening if the competitor cannot
or will not close it.

---

# How to Apply It Here

**Ask why the weakness exists before treating it as an opening.** A gap in a mature product is usually a decision.
Finding the decision tells you whether it will be revisited.

**Look for the commercial conflict specifically.** A competitor charging per seat cannot easily ship the feature that
reduces seats. One funded by professional services cannot ship the thing that removes the services. These are the
weaknesses that persist for years.

**Distinguish neglect from inability.** A large supplier ignoring single-handed practices is neglect, and it reverses
if the segment becomes attractive. `Threats.md` covers what that reversal looks like.

**Attach a duration to architectural weaknesses.** "They would have to rebuild their data model" is strong for two or
three years, not forever — and funding changes the timeline.

**Read the support forum and the reviews for the persistent complaint.** Friction unfixed after several years is
structural nearly by definition, and `04-problem`'s `Feedback.md` covers how to weigh it.

---

# Where It Misleads

**Fixable weaknesses are the easiest to find and the most tempting to build on.** A missing feature, a dated
interface, a clumsy flow — all real, all closable in a quarter by a competitor who notices. Building a company on one
is the failure `Gap-Analysis.md` exists to prevent.

**Weaknesses are inferred from marketing rather than from use.** What a site does not mention may still exist, and
what it advertises may barely work. The evidence is in changelogs, forums and reviews.

**Old complaints describe a product that has changed.** Reviews from three years ago name friction that may be
fixed. Date every citation, as `04-problem` requires.

**A weakness for one segment is a strength for another.** A product that is complex because it is configurable is
weak for solo users and strong for large organizations. State the segment with the weakness or the claim is
meaningless.

**Their weakness is assumed to be our strength.** It becomes one only if this product actually addresses it and can
hold it — which is two further questions, answered in the coverage table and the defensibility test.
