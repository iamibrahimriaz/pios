# Indirect Competitors

---

# What It Is

Everything competing for the problem that is not a direct competitor — and this is where the market actually
sits.

| Type | What it is | Why it wins |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Indirect** | Solves the same or an adjacent problem for a different segment | Buyers stretch a tool they already own rather than add one |
| **Substitute** | Solves it by an entirely different mechanism | Hiring a person, outsourcing, a service instead of software |
| **Status quo** | Whatever they do today — paper, a spreadsheet, an assistant, memory | Free, installed, trusted, requires no change |
| **Non-consumption** | They have the problem and chose nothing | Inertia costs nothing and is never wrong |

The gate requires the status quo to be evaluated as a competitor. It is usually the strongest one in the market,
and it never appears in a category search.

---

# When It Applies

In Move 1 and Move 2, and scored in Move 3 like everything else. The status quo has its own column in the coverage
table.

---

# How to Apply It Here

**Score the status quo against the ranked problems properly.** It frequently scores "partly" on the top problem —
which is the honest reason a market has stayed as it is. Recording it as "not" to make the opening look larger
invalidates the whole table.

**Price the status quo at zero and count its switching cost.** `03-user` priced leaving it across five dimensions.
That figure is the actual bar this product must clear, and it is a higher bar than any competitor's feature list.

**Treat non-consumption as a strategy question, not an absence.** If most of the segment uses nothing, the
competition is inertia — and `11-growth` needs to know, because education and trigger-based acquisition are
entirely different from displacement.

**Include the general-purpose tools.** A spreadsheet, a notes app, email and a shared drive solve a remarkable share
of professional problems adequately. They are the substitute most often omitted and the hardest to displace, because
they are already paid for.

**Look for the human substitute.** An assistant, a transcription service, a colleague who "just knows". These
compete on trust and flexibility, and they set a quality bar an automated product has to meet rather than approach.

---

# Where It Misleads

**"They aren't really competitors" is how the strongest competitor leaves the analysis.** A spreadsheet is not in
the category and does not market itself, and it holds the account.

**Non-consumption is read as untapped demand.** It is more often demonstrated tolerance: the problem exists and has
not provoked action. That is a harder market, not a larger one, and `04-problem`'s workaround dimension already
flagged it.

**The status quo's advantages are underestimated because they are invisible.** Nobody lists "requires no change",
"already trusted", "no procurement", "no training", "no risk". Every one of those is a feature this product lacks
on day one.

**Substitutes get excluded on grounds of being low-tech.** The mechanism is irrelevant; what matters is whether the
person's problem is addressed. A £200-a-month typist competes directly with an AI documentation tool.

**Indirect competitors become direct ones.** A tool serving an adjacent segment can extend into this one cheaply,
and `Threats.md` covers that path. Classification here is a snapshot.
