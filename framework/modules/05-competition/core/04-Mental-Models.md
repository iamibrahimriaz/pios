# Mental Models

---

# Model Statement

> Competitive analysis is not a survey of companies.
>
> It is an argument about where a new entrant could stand, and for how long.

---

# 1. The Competitive Set the Buyer Uses

**Reveals:** who you are actually compared against.

Buyers do not compare products by category taxonomy. They compare the two or three options
they happened to hear about, plus what they already do.

Find that set: "alternatives to X" pages, forum threads asking for recommendations, the
options named in reviews as "we also looked at".

**Hides:** it is a snapshot of current awareness, not of capability. A better product
nobody has heard of is not in the set — which is itself a finding about distribution.

---

# 2. The Status Quo as Incumbent

**Reveals:** the real bar for adoption.

Treat "what they do today" as a competitor with a full profile: what it costs, what it is
good at, why it persists, and what would displace it.

Its strengths are formidable and rarely written down — free, installed, trusted, no
migration, no approval needed, and nobody gets blamed for continuing.

**Hides:** status quo strength varies by person. The same workflow is tolerable to one
practitioner and intolerable to another; segment carefully.

---

# 3. Non-Consumption

**Reveals:** whether the barrier is competition or inertia.

People with the problem who use nothing have made a decision. Understanding why is
strategically different from understanding why someone chose a competitor.

| If most of the segment... | Then the strategy is... |
| --- | --- |
| Uses a competitor | Displacement — be better, be worth switching |
| Uses nothing | Education and triggers — make the problem visible |

**Hides:** non-consumption can mean the problem is not painful enough. That is a module 04
finding surfacing late, and it should be sent back rather than explained away.

---

# 4. Problem Coverage, Not Feature Coverage

**Reveals:** where the market fails the person you chose.

A matrix of competitors against ranked problems produces a conclusion. A matrix of
competitors against features produces a document.

**Hides:** it depends entirely on module 04 having ranked the right problems. If the
problem set is wrong, this table is confidently wrong.

---

# 5. Jobs the Competitor Was Hired For

**Reveals:** why an incumbent is entrenched despite obvious weaknesses.

A product users complain about constantly may be doing one job so well that nothing else
matters — usually compliance, integration, or being what everyone already knows.

Ask: what job is this competitor uniquely hired for? That job is what you would have to
match, not the features people complain about.

**Hides:** it can overstate entrenchment. Sometimes a product persists purely through
inertia and no job at all.

---

# 6. The Six-Month Question

**Reveals:** whether an opening is a business or a feature.

> If this works, what stops the incumbent shipping it in six months?

Work through their constraints:

| Constraint | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| It cannibalizes their revenue | Strong |
| They lack the data | Strong while it lasts |
| It needs a different sales motion | Moderate — they can build, not sell |
| Their existing customers would object | Moderate |
| Nothing — we would just be faster | Weak, sometimes sufficient |

**Hides:** incumbents are often slower than this model assumes. A weak moat plus real
speed has built many companies. The point is to know which one you have.

---

# 7. The Changelog Lens

**Reveals:** structural weakness, evidenced by their own spending.

Read a year of release notes. What gets fixed repeatedly is what cannot be fixed properly —
a limitation of their architecture, their data model, or their business model.

What ships first after funding reveals strategy more reliably than any roadmap.

**Hides:** not every company publishes meaningful release notes, and absence tells you
little.

---

# 8. Price as a Statement of Intent

**Reveals:** who a product was built for.

A product at ten times the price of another is not competing for the same buyer, whatever
their feature lists suggest.

| Signal | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| "Contact us" pricing | Sales-led, price floor, negotiated — a committee buyer |
| Low flat rate | Self-serve, volume, minimal support |
| Free tier | Funded by something else — find out what |
| Usage-based | Aligned to customer scale; revenue is unpredictable |

**Hides:** price is set by strategy and history, not only by value. A legacy price point
may reflect a decision made years ago.

---

# 9. The Graveyard

**Reveals:** what has already been learned, at someone else's cost.

Failed predecessors are the most informative competitors, because their outcome is known.

Find why they stopped. The reason is usually structural — buyers would not pay, the
workaround sufficed, the sale required approval nobody would give.

**Hides:** timing. A product that failed in 2016 may have been right and early. Establish
whether the killing constraint still holds before treating it as fate.

---

# 10. The Inversion

**Reveals:** whether the market is actually well served.

Instead of "where is the gap?", ask: **"what would I expect to see if this market were
already well served — and am I seeing it?"**

Signals: mature products with satisfied reviewers, low switching activity, complaints that
are cosmetic rather than structural, no active workarounds circulating.

**Hides:** nothing. It is the correction for a module whose entire purpose creates
pressure to find an opening.

---

# Combining Models

| Situation | Model |
| --- | --- |
| Identifying who you are compared to | The Competitive Set the Buyer Uses |
| Setting the real adoption bar | The Status Quo as Incumbent |
| Deciding displacement vs education | Non-Consumption |
| Finding the opening | Problem Coverage |
| Understanding entrenchment | Jobs the Competitor Was Hired For |
| Testing durability | The Six-Month Question |
| Finding structural weakness | The Changelog Lens |
| Reading strategy from pricing | Price as a Statement of Intent |
| Learning cheaply | The Graveyard |
| Checking yourself | The Inversion |

Apply Problem Coverage to find the opening. Apply The Six-Month Question to test it. Apply
The Inversion to whatever survives.

---

# Self Assessment

- Do I know the comparison set the buyer actually uses?
- Did I profile the status quo as seriously as a funded competitor?
- Do I know whether the barrier is competition or inertia?
- Did I score on problems rather than features?
- Do I know what job each incumbent is uniquely hired for?
- Did I answer the six-month question with evidence?
- Did I read changelogs?
- Did I check the graveyard, and whether its lesson still holds?
- What would I see if this market were well served — and am I seeing it?
