# Best Practices

---

# 1. Start From the Buyer's Comparison Set

Before running a category search, find what buyers actually compare: "alternatives to
«leading product»" pages, forum threads asking for recommendations, and the "we also
evaluated" line in reviews.

That set is usually smaller and stranger than the category, and it is the one you have to
win.

---

# 2. Give the Status Quo a Full Profile

Write its row like a competitor's: what it costs, what it is good at, who it serves, why
it persists, what would displace it.

Writing "paper and after-hours transcription" in the strengths column forces the honest
observation that it is free, requires no approval, and has never lost anyone's data to an
outage.

---

# 3. Read a Year of Changelogs

Repeated fixes in one area mark a structural weakness — evidenced by the competitor's own
engineering spend.

What they ship first after funding or a leadership change reveals strategy more reliably
than any published roadmap.

---

# 4. Read Reviews for Capability, Homepages for Claims

Never let a homepage populate a capability column.

Record both when they disagree — "claims real-time; reviews describe 15-minute delays" is
a stronger finding than either statement alone.

---

# 5. Date Every Price

`[verified: pricing page, accessed 2026-04-02]` stays useful for years because a reader
knows how stale it is.

The same claim undated becomes unusable within months and cannot be safely refreshed.

---

# 6. Treat "Contact Us" as Data

It is not a missing price. It tells you: sales-led motion, a floor below which they will
not go, negotiated contracts, and a buyer who expects to negotiate.

That profile alone often rules them out as a competitor for a self-serve segment.

---

# 7. Find Out How Free Products Are Funded

A free competitor is funded by something — ads, an open core, a loss leader, an upsell, or
a parent company's strategy.

That funding model predicts their next move, and whether they can sustain being free.

---

# 8. Search for Shutdowns, Not Launches

Query "shutting down", "sunset", "no longer maintained", "acquired and discontinued", plus
category vocabulary.

Then establish why. And record your search terms — "no abandoned attempts found, searched
X, Y, Z" is a finding; silence is not.

---

# 9. Ask What Each Incumbent Is Uniquely Hired For

A widely criticized product often survives because it does one thing nothing else does —
usually compliance, an integration, or being the thing everyone already knows.

That is what you would actually have to match. The complaints are noise by comparison.

---

# 10. Answer the Six-Month Question in Writing

Not as a feeling. Work through their business model, pricing structure, sales motion, and
customer base, and write the answer down.

"They could build it in a quarter; they could not sell it without a new motion" is a
strategy input. "We have first-mover advantage" is not.

---

# 11. Write "Not Defensible" When It Is True

The pressure in this module is to find and protect an opening.

A clear statement that the gap could be closed quickly, with speed and focus as the only
advantages, is more useful than an invented moat — because module 07 can plan for a short
window and cannot plan for an imaginary one.

---

# 12. Test Positioning Against a Competitor's Homepage

Write the statement, then read it as though it appeared on the market leader's site.

If it would fit there without anyone noticing, it is a category description. Rewrite.

---

# 13. Keep the Problem Table Bigger Than the Feature Table

If the feature comparison is longer than the problem coverage table, the analysis has
drifted into cataloging.

Feature tables establish table stakes. Problem tables locate opportunity. Spend effort
accordingly.

---

# 14. Note What You Could Not Assess

A competitor whose pricing is private and whose reviews are thin should be marked as
partially assessed, not quietly given a confident row.

Analysis quality is judged by whether a reader can tell which rows are solid.

---

# 15. Stop Before Deciding

When the analysis starts proposing what to build in response, stop. That is module 07.

This module establishes what exists, where the opening is, and how long it might last.
Deciding is a separate act, made with the business model in hand.

---

# Anti-Practices

| Habit | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Category search only | Misses substitutes, status quo, non-consumption |
| Status quo listed but unscored | Understates the hardest competitor |
| Feature table as the main artifact | Catalogues instead of concluding |
| Homepage claims in capability columns | Marketing mistaken for evidence |
| Undated pricing | Unusable within months |
| Silent price estimates | A guess becomes a revenue model |
| Gap asserted with no cause | Usually imaginary |
| Skipping the six-month question | Builds a feature and calls it a company |
| Superlative positioning | Anyone can claim it |

---

# Self Assessment

- Did I start from the buyer's comparison set?
- Did I write the status quo's strengths, not just its weaknesses?
- Did I read changelogs and reviews, not just homepages?
- Is every price sourced and dated?
- Did I search for shutdowns?
- Did I answer the six-month question in writing?
- Would my positioning look out of place on a competitor's site?
- Did I mark what I could not assess?

---

> **Practice Principle**
>
> Competitors publish everything except the one thing you need:
> whether they could take your opening if they noticed it.
>
> That answer has to be built, from their business model outward.
