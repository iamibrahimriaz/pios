# Core Principles

---

# Principle Statement

> Your competition is not a list of companies.
>
> It is everything the user could do instead of buying from you —
> including nothing.

---

# Principle 1 — The Status Quo Is the Incumbent

Whatever the user does today is a competitor, and usually the strongest one.

It is free. It is already installed. It is fully trusted. It requires no migration, no
training, no approval, and nobody gets blamed for continuing it.

A product must not merely be better than the status quo. It must be better by enough to
justify disruption — a far higher bar, and the one most new products fail.

---

# Principle 2 — Doing Nothing Is a Competitive Choice

People who have the problem and use nothing are not an untapped market. They are a market
that has already decided.

If most of the segment sits here, the competition is inertia rather than any product, and
the strategy that follows is completely different — education and trigger events rather
than comparison and displacement.

---

# Principle 3 — Compare on Problems, Not Features

Feature checklists look thorough and decide nothing. Given time, everyone ships everything.

Score competitors against the ranked problems from module 04. That answers the question
that matters: for the person we chose to serve, which of their real problems does nobody
solve well?

Feature comparison has exactly one legitimate use — establishing table stakes.

---

# Principle 4 — Vendors Describe Intent, Users Describe Capability

A homepage says what a company wants to be true. A three-star review says what happens
when someone uses it on a Tuesday.

Record marketing claims as claims. When the claim and the reviews disagree, the
disagreement is the finding.

---

# Principle 5 — Changelogs Do Not Lie

What a company fixes repeatedly is what it cannot fix structurally.

A release note history is a competitor's engineering budget, spent in public, on the
problems they consider urgent. It is the most honest document any vendor publishes.

---

# Principle 6 — A Gap Needs a Reason

When a market leaves an obvious need unserved, there is almost always a cause: regulation,
economics, distribution, data access, or misaligned incentives.

Find the cause. It tells you whether the gap is an opportunity or a trap that has already
caught someone.

A gap with no explanation usually means the search stopped early.

---

# Principle 7 — Enterable Is Not Holdable

The question that separates competitive analysis from competitive strategy:

> If this works, what stops the incumbent shipping it in six months?

Most analysis never asks it. An opening anyone can close is a feature, not a company —
and the plan built on it needs to know it is racing a clock.

---

# Principle 8 — "Not Defensible" Is a Finding, Not a Failure

Many good businesses begin with no moat. Speed, focus, and a willing first segment are
sometimes enough.

But it must be a known condition entering module 07, not something discovered after
launch. State it plainly. A strategy can be built around a short window; it cannot be
built around an imagined one.

---

# Principle 9 — Price Without a Source Becomes a Business Model

A figure written here as "around $50" is read by module 06 as an input, and by module 07
as a fact.

Source it and date it, or mark it unavailable. Never estimate silently.

---

# Principle 10 — Positioning a Competitor Could Claim Is Not Positioning

"The easiest way to do X" is a category description. Anyone can say it, and most do.

If the leading competitor could put your positioning statement on their homepage without
lying, it is not differentiation. Rewrite until only this product could say it.

---

# Principle 11 — Search the Graveyard First

If a problem is obvious and valuable, someone tried. Find out what happened.

The reason a predecessor stopped is rarely "bad execution". It is usually structural — the
buyers would not pay, the workaround was adequate, the sale needed a committee.

That lesson was paid for by someone else. Refusing to collect it is the most expensive
kind of thrift.

---

# Principle Hierarchy

```
Who competes — including what is not a product
   ↓
How well they serve the ranked problems
   ↓
At what price
   ↓
Where the opening is
   ↓
Whether it can be held
   ↓
What we claim
```

Positioning derived before defensibility is a marketing statement about a race that may
already be lost.

---

# Common Violations

- Listing only funded software products.
- Mentioning the status quo without scoring it.
- Assuming everyone in the segment uses something.
- Building a feature table instead of a problem-coverage table.
- Quoting vendor marketing as established capability.
- Asserting a gap with no structural reason.
- Skipping the six-month question.
- Estimating a price without saying so.
- Writing positioning any competitor could claim.
- Never searching for who tried and stopped.

---

# Self Assessment

- Did I include competitors that are not companies?
- Did I take the status quo seriously enough to describe its strengths?
- Did I score on problems, or fall back on features?
- Did I separate what vendors claim from what users report?
- Does my gap have a stated reason for existing?
- Did I answer the six-month question honestly?
- Is every price sourced and dated?
- Could a competitor claim my positioning?
