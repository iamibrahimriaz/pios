# Reflection

---

# Overview

You wrote an analysis of people you intend to beat. These questions are about how much
of that intention got into the analysis.

---

# On the Set

**Who did you leave out, and why?** There is usually one — either too big to think
about, or too small to seem serious. Both omissions are informative.

**Did you profile the status quo, or mention it?** A row in a table is a mention.

**Which competitor did you spend the most time on?** Frequently it is the most
interesting one rather than the most threatening one. Those are rarely the same
company.

---

# On the Comparison

**Who chose the rows?** If the answer is "we did, from our roadmap," the table is a
description of your plan with competitors' names on it.

**Which row did you lose?** If none, the exercise did not happen. Every real product
loses something to every real competitor.

**Where did you write "partial" or "limited"?** Those words usually appear where the
competitor does the thing adequately and you would rather not say so.

---

# On the Incumbent

**Do you understand why their worst flaw exists?** If your explanation is that they are
careless or slow, you do not yet.

**What are they good at that you will not match?** Every incumbent has something —
distribution, integrations, trust, an installed base, a certification. Naming it is
more useful than any list of their failings.

**If they noticed you tomorrow, what would they do?** The answer is often "nothing for
two years," which is a real advantage worth stating explicitly rather than assuming
silently.

---

# On the Gap

**Why is it empty?** Ask again. The first answer is usually the flattering one.

**Has anyone tried this before?** A gap that was attempted and abandoned is the most
informative kind, and the attempt is usually findable.

**What would it take for a competitor to close it after you prove it works?** If the
answer is "a quarter," your plan needs to say what happens then.

---

# On Your Own Pattern

| Look for | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Your matrices always favor you | The dimensions are chosen, not derived |
| You never profile the status quo | You compete with products rather than with behavior |
| Your gaps are always openings | Absence reads as opportunity to you |
| Your advantages are always moats | You are describing the present as though it were durable |
| You avoid the largest competitor | Analysis is being shaped by what is comfortable to consider |

---

# The Question Worth Returning To

> If you were advising the incumbent, what would you tell them to do about you?

Answering this seriously produces two useful things: a realistic estimate of how long
your opening stays open, and an unusually clear view of where you are actually weak.

Most teams have never asked it, and it takes ten minutes.

---

> **Reflection Principle**
>
> You are the least neutral reader of your own competitive analysis.
>
> The corrective is not more research. It is deliberately taking the other side for
> long enough to notice what you have been arranging around.
