# Evaluation

---

# Overview

The test underneath all of these: **does your analysis contain anything you did not
want to find?**

---

# Exercise 1 — Lose a Row

Take a comparison matrix you have written. Add three rows chosen by someone who prefers
a competitor.

**Passing looks like.** You lose at least one, and it is a row that matters.

**Failing looks like.** You still win everything. The dimensions were selected, and
adding rows chosen by an advocate is the fastest way to expose it.

---

# Exercise 2 — Profile the Spreadsheet

Take any product category and write the status quo's competitor profile — advantages
first, at least five of them, before any weakness.

**Passing looks like.** The profile is genuinely persuasive. You should finish it
slightly less sure of the product.

**Failing looks like.** The advantages run out after two. Look again — zero cost, zero
learning, perfect fit, total trust, complete flexibility, and no procurement process are
six before you start.

---

# Exercise 3 — Explain a Persistent Flaw

Pick a widely-criticized feature of a major product you use. Explain why it still exists,
in terms of what fixing it would cost the company.

**Passing looks like.** A plausible business reason — a larger segment served, an
architectural cost, a protected revenue line.

**Failing looks like.** "They don't care about users." That is never the explanation and
believing it is how a competitor's deliberate choice gets mistaken for an oversight.

---

# Exercise 4 — Classify Five Gaps

Find five things nobody in a category offers. Assign each to: nobody has, nobody can,
nobody should.

**Passing looks like.** At least three land in "nobody should."

**Failing looks like.** Most land in "nobody has." That distribution is almost never
real in a market older than a few years.

---

# Exercise 5 — Kill Your Moat

Write your durable advantage. Then write how a competitor with three times your
resources neutralizes it in six months.

**Passing looks like.** You cannot write a convincing neutralization. That is rare and
it means you have something.

**Failing looks like.** The neutralization is easy and obvious. That is the normal
result, and it reclassifies the advantage as a lead — which is workable, and requires a
different plan.

---

# Exercise 6 — Position Against a Name

Write a positioning statement containing: the specific alternative you displace, the
specific person, and the one dimension you win on.

**Passing looks like.** Someone could disagree with it.

**Failing looks like.** It would fit three products in the category. Interchangeable
positioning is a description with adjectives.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Lists competitors and their weaknesses; no status quo row |
| **Working** | Profiles the status quo, but as a weak option |
| **Competent** | Explains why incumbent flaws persist; classifies gaps with reasoning; captures or marks pricing honestly |
| **Fluent** | Produces an analysis that removed one of their own options, and calls a lead a lead |

---

# A Note on What Cannot Be Assessed Here

Whether the opening is worth pursuing. A defensible gap can sit in front of a segment
that cannot pay, in a market whose timing is wrong, at a price that does not clear the
cost to serve. Modules 06, 07 and 13 each test a different part of that.

A strong gap analysis is the most persuasive artifact produced up to this point in a
run, and persuasion is exactly what makes it dangerous to treat as a decision.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> A competitive analysis is working when it makes a plan harder rather than easier.
>
> If yours has never closed off an option, it has not yet been used for anything.
