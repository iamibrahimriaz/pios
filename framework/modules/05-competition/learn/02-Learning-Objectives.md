# Learning Objectives

---

# Overview

> After this module you should be able to explain why an incumbent's worst flaw still
> exists, score the status quo as though it were funded, and state one opening you could
> defend for longer than two quarters.

---

# Knowledge Objectives

You should understand:

- The difference between a direct competitor, an indirect one, and a substitute
- Why the status quo qualifies as a competitor, and how to profile it
- The three reasons a gap is empty
- What a moat is, and the difference between a real one and a head start
- Why feature comparison tables are the least informative part of the analysis
- What positioning is: a named alternative, a named audience, and a named dimension

---

# Thinking Objectives

The shift is from *who else does this* to *why does the current arrangement persist*.

Instead of asking:

> "Who are our competitors and what are they missing?"

ask:

- Who has this budget today, and what is it spent on?
- Why has this obvious flaw survived five years of complaints?
- What would it cost the incumbent to fix it, and what would it cost them if they did?
- Why is this space empty?
- If we win, what stops them copying it in a quarter?

The fourth and fifth are the ones that separate analysis from cataloging.

---

# Skill Objectives

You should be able to:

- Assemble a competitor set that includes indirect competitors and the status quo
- Capture pricing, or mark it explicitly unavailable rather than estimating it
- Profile the status quo with its genuine advantages listed first
- Classify a gap into one of the three explanations, with reasoning
- Write positioning that names an alternative rather than describing a product
- Distinguish a durable advantage from a temporary one

---

# Analytical Objectives

You should develop the ability to:

- Read a competitor's pricing page as a statement about their target customer
- Notice when a comparison table's rows were chosen because you win them
- Recognize a "competitor" that serves a different job entirely
- Tell a moat from a lead — and be honest that most advantages are leads

---

# Judgment Objectives

**Which gap to pursue.** The gate requires one defensible gap. Defensible is the word
doing the work — it means you can hold it, not merely reach it first.

**How to weight the status quo.** Scoring it fairly often makes it the strongest entry
in the matrix, which is uncomfortable and frequently correct. The judgment is whether
your product beats it by more than the switching cost from module 03.

---

# You Have Learned This When

| Signal | What it indicates |
| --- | --- |
| Your first competitor entry is the status quo | The habit has formed |
| You can explain why an incumbent's worst flaw persists | You are analyzing the business, not the interface |
| You have concluded "nobody should" about a gap | Absence is no longer read as opportunity |
| Your positioning names a specific alternative | It is positioning rather than description |
| You call your advantage a lead when it is one | The moat distinction is honest |

The last one is the hardest. Almost every early advantage is a lead, and most
competitive analyses call it a moat.

---

# What This Module Does Not Teach You

It does not teach pricing strategy — that is module 06, using this module's comparison.
It does not choose the approach — module 07 does. It does not tell you whether the
market is worth entering; that verdict is assembled and no single module owns it.

---

> **Objectives Principle**
>
> The skill is explaining why the current arrangement persists.
>
> Anyone can list what competitors lack. Understanding why they lack it is what
> distinguishes an opening from a hole.
