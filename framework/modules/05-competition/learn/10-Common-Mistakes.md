# Common Mistakes

---

# Overview

Competitive analysis fails in one direction: toward flattery. Almost every mistake below
makes the product look better placed than it is.

---

# 1. The Table Whose Rows You Win

**What it looks like.** A feature matrix with ten rows. Your column has nine checks;
the nearest competitor has four.

**Why it is tempting.** You choose the rows, and you choose them from your own
roadmap — so of course you win them.

**What it costs.** Module 07 chooses an approach against this analysis. A matrix scored
on dimensions selected for your advantage confirms whatever the team already intended
and cannot surface a reason to change direction.

**Instead.** Choose rows from the ranked problems in module 04, not from your feature
list. If you lose some, the table is doing its job.

---

# 2. The Status Quo Omitted

**What it looks like.** Five funded competitors, carefully profiled. No row for the
spreadsheet.

**Why it is tempting.** It is not a company. There is nothing to research, no pricing
page, no funding round.

**What it costs.** This is the gate's explicit requirement, and skipping it is the most
consequential omission in the module. Most launches into a "greenfield" market fail to
the status quo, not to a rival — and the analysis contains no record that it was ever
considered.

**Instead.** Give it a full profile: cost, fit, trust, flexibility, and switching cost
of zero. Module 03 already documented it.

---

# 3. Competitors Listed, Not Understood

**What it looks like.** Name, funding, headcount, a summary of the product, a bullet
list of weaknesses.

**Why it is tempting.** This information is available in an afternoon and looks like
research.

**What it costs.** None of it explains why the weakness persists, which is the only
part that predicts whether it will still be there when you launch.

**Instead.** For each competitor, answer one question: what would it cost them to fix
their most-cited flaw, and what would fixing it cost them elsewhere?

---

# 4. Pricing Estimated Instead of Captured

**What it looks like.** "Approximately $50/user/month."

**Why it is tempting.** Enterprise pricing is often unpublished, and a figure is needed
for the comparison.

**What it costs.** Module 06 justifies its price against these figures. An invented
comparison produces a price that is justified against nothing, and the error is
invisible because it looks like every other row.

**Instead.** Capture what is published, and mark the rest explicitly unavailable. The
gate permits "unavailable." It does not permit a guess that looks like a finding.

---

# 5. Every Gap Read as an Opening

**What it looks like.** "No competitor offers X" appearing in the gap analysis with no
further comment.

**Why it is tempting.** An empty cell looks like opportunity, and the alternative
explanations are deflating.

**What it costs.** Module 07 builds strategy on the gap. If it is empty because nobody
wants the thing, the strategy is aimed at a hole and every subsequent module elaborates
it.

**Instead.** For each gap, state which of the three reasons applies and why. Most will
be "nobody should," and finding that out costs an afternoon rather than a year.

---

# 6. A Lead Called a Moat

**What it looks like.** "Our advantage is our superior user experience and faster
iteration."

**Why it is tempting.** It is true right now, and it feels like a strategy.

**What it costs.** Neither is defensible. A competitor with more engineers can match
both, and module 07 will have built a plan whose central assumption expires when
somebody notices.

**Instead.** Ask what still protects you after a well-resourced competitor decides to
copy it. Usually the honest answer is a lead measured in months, which is workable if
you plan for it and fatal if you plan around it.

---

# 7. The Competitor Who Is Not One

**What it looks like.** A well-known product in the same broad category, profiled at
length.

**Why it is tempting.** They are the obvious name, and leaving them out feels like an
omission.

**What it costs.** Attention spent on a company serving a different job for a different
buyer, while the actual alternative — often a general-purpose tool or a person — goes
unprofiled.

**Instead.** Test membership against module 04's ranked problems. A competitor is
something the user could choose *instead*, for the same job.

---

# The Pattern

| Mistake | Underlying move |
| --- | --- |
| Rows you win | Choosing the dimensions of comparison |
| Status quo omitted | Only counting competitors with a website |
| Listing without understanding | Collecting what is available |
| Pricing estimated | Filling a cell rather than marking it empty |
| Gaps as openings | Absence read as evidence |
| Lead called a moat | Present advantage mistaken for durable one |
| The famous non-competitor | Category membership mistaken for competition |

All seven produce the same document: one where the opening is real, the incumbents are
weak, and the plan is sound. That document is the module's characteristic output when
it has not been done properly, and it is indistinguishable from a good one at a glance.

---

> **Mistakes Principle**
>
> A competitive analysis that makes you feel better about the plan has probably not
> been done.
>
> The useful version is the one that removes an option.
