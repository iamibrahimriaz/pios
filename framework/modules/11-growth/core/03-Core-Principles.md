# Core Principles

---

# Principle Statement

> Growth is a property of the product, not an activity performed on it.
>
> This module's job is to name the property this product has — and to say so
> when the answer is "none yet".

---

# Principle 1 — The Model Comes Before the Tactics

Sales-led, product-led, community-led, marketplace, referral. These demand different products,
different prices and different teams.

Tactics chosen without a model are a list of activities. The model is what makes any of them
coherent, and it is a product decision rather than a marketing one.

---

# Principle 2 — Check the Arithmetic Before Planning Anything

Margin per user times an acceptable payback period gives a CAC ceiling. The cost of one unit of
the motion, divided by conversions, gives its implied CAC.

If the second exceeds the first, the motion is not affordable at this price, and no amount of
planning changes that. A £20-a-month product cannot fund a field sales conversation.

---

# Principle 3 — Never Raise a Conversion Assumption to Make the Arithmetic Pass

This is the single most common way an unaffordable motion survives into a funding decision.

If the payback check fails, the price, the segment or the model has to change — and that is a
regress, recorded.

---

# Principle 4 — A Channel Is a Factual Claim

Saying users are reachable somewhere is a claim about where people are. It can be evidenced, and
it must be: cite where the segment was observed.

A channel with no citation was chosen by convention, which is how a plan ends up marketing to
people who are not there.

---

# Principle 5 — A Competitor's Channel Is Not Proof It Works

That they advertise somewhere establishes that they advertise there.

Unprofitable channels persist for years, and profitability is not visible from outside. This is
the same caution `05-competition` applies to vendor marketing.

---

# Principle 6 — Choose the First Channel by Learning Cost

Projected CAC before launch is an assumption. The cost of finding out is a fact.

Compare channels on what one test costs and how quickly it produces a usable answer, not on
modeled volume.

---

# Principle 7 — Activation Is an Action That Proves Value

"Signed up" is not activation. The test: if a user did this and never returned, would you still
claim they received value? If yes, it is the wrong event.

An event chosen for being easy to log measures nothing, and it is the event most likely to be
chosen.

---

# Principle 8 — The Switching Cost Sits Inside Activation

Migration, retraining, running in parallel. These are the largest things standing between a user
and first value.

Placing them after activation is how activation rates get planned optimistically and then
disappoint for reasons that were written down two modules earlier.

---

# Principle 9 — Acquisition Without Activation Is Churn With Extra Steps

Spending to reach users before the activation path works buys disappointment at retail price.

The sequence is not a preference. Each stage has a condition that must be true before it starts.

---

# Principle 10 — "A Great Product" Is Not a Retention Mechanism

Accumulated data, habit, workflow dependency, network, switching cost. Those are mechanisms.
They make leaving costly, which is what retention means mechanically.

---

# Principle 11 — Every Retention Mechanism Names a Requirement

A mechanism is a claim about the product, so it is checkable against `08-product` directly.

A mechanism with no requirement behind it is a hope. A mechanism delivered by deferred scope
means retention is unproven at launch — a legitimate position, and very different from what the
plan otherwise implies.

---

# Principle 12 — The Early Warning Beats the Rate

Churn percentage tells you what already happened. The behavior that precedes leaving is the only
thing anyone can act on.

Name it. This is the same principle `07-strategy` applied to risks, and for the same reason.

---

# Principle 13 — Apply the Closure Test, Mechanically

Does the output become the next input? That question has an answer, and it is not a matter of
framing.

If yes, it is a loop and it compounds. If no, it is a funnel and it scales linearly with spend.

---

# Principle 14 — A Funnel Is Legitimate. A Funnel Drawn as a Loop Is Not

Many good businesses grow through a funnel.

An arrow curving back to the top of one claims compounding the business does not have, and it
changes decisions about spend, hiring and valuation made by someone reading in good faith. A
diagram is a claim.

---

# Principle 15 — Inherited Uncertainty Stays Uncertain

CAC and churn arrived unknown from `06-business`, handled with a sensitivity table rather than a
figure.

A new document is not new evidence. Restating them as per-channel numbers is laundering, and it
is how a financial model comes to rest on figures nobody could support.

---

# Principle 16 — Referral Is a Finding, Not a Design Choice

Whether a segment refers depends on professional norms: peers share practice, competitors do
not, and some recommendations carry reputational risk.

Designing a referral program for a segment the research says will not refer is not an execution
problem.

---

# Principle 17 — Name the First Ten

Not a market description. Named people where possible, specific profiles otherwise, with the
route, their reason, and the objection.

Where the problem is assumed, these ten are the validation sample rather than a sales pipeline —
and saying so is what keeps the first conversations honest.

---

# Principle 18 — No Spend Before Validation

Where the sharpest problem is assumed, acquisition spend waits for Milestone Zero.

This is the fourth place that mechanism appears, and the first where it costs money rather than
time. Spending to acquire users for an unverified problem amplifies uncertainty instead of
resolving it.

---

# Principle Hierarchy

```
Model chosen, and checked against the price
   ↓
Channels evidenced, first one chosen by learning cost
   ↓
Activation defined as an action that proves value
   ↓
Retention located in the product
   ↓
Compounding tested, honestly
   ↓
First ten named
   ↓
Spending sequenced behind what must be true
```

Each level depends on the one above. Channels chosen before a model suit no particular motion;
loops described before retention assume compounding on top of churn.

---

# Common Violations

- Tactics with no model behind them.
- Skipping the payback check.
- Raising a conversion rate until the arithmetic passes.
- Channels with no evidence the segment is there.
- Competitor spend cited as proof.
- First channel chosen by projected CAC.
- Activation defined as a signup.
- Switching cost placed after activation.
- Retention resting on product quality.
- Mechanisms with no requirement behind them.
- Churn stated without its early warning.
- Funnels presented as loops.
- CAC and churn restated more confidently than in `06-business`.
- Referral programs for segments that will not refer.
- The first ten described as a market.
- Spend planned before validation.

---

# Self Assessment

- Did I choose a model before choosing tactics?
- Does the motion survive the payback arithmetic?
- Did I bend an assumption to make it pass?
- Does every channel cite an observation?
- Did I choose the first channel by what a test costs?
- Would my activation event count someone who never returned?
- Does the switching cost sit inside activation?
- Does every retention mechanism name a requirement?
- Did I name the behavior that precedes leaving?
- Does anything actually close?
- Is my CAC position as uncertain as module 06's?
- Are the first ten real?
- Is spending gated on validation?
