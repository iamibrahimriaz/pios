# Mental Models

---

# Model Statement

> A growth plan is the easiest document in the framework to write well
> and the hardest to write honestly.
>
> These lenses are ways of finding the sentences that only sound like findings.

---

# 1. The Payback Arithmetic

**Reveals:** whether the motion is possible at this price.

Margin per user times payback months against the cost of one unit of the motion divided by its
conversions. Two numbers, compared.

It is the most decisive lens here, because the ceiling comes from someone else's document and
cannot be argued with by anyone fluent.

**Hides:** it assumes the price is fixed. Sometimes the right answer is a different price, which
is a regress rather than a failure.

---

# 2. Where Were They Standing?

**Reveals:** borrowed growth.

For each channel, ask: where in the research was this segment actually observed? Name the
interview, the publication, the association, the gathering.

Channels that survive are the ones with a person behind them. Channels that do not are the ones
imported from another product's playbook.

**Hides:** a segment can be somewhere and still not be reachable there. Presence is necessary,
not sufficient.

---

# 3. The Cost of Finding Out

**Reveals:** which channel to try first.

Not projected CAC — that is an assumption. What does one test cost, and how long until it gives a
usable answer?

Reframing the comparison this way changes the order almost every time, because the cheapest
channel to *learn from* is rarely the one with the best modeled economics.

**Hides:** a cheap test can produce an ambiguous answer. Ask what result would actually decide
anything.

---

# 4. The User Who Never Came Back

**Reveals:** activation events chosen for convenience.

Take the proposed activation event. Imagine a user who did exactly that and never returned.
Would you still claim they received value?

If yes, the event measures a step in your funnel rather than a moment in their work.

**Hides:** it validates the event, not the rate. A perfect event with a 3% rate is still a
problem.

---

# 5. The Cost of Leaving

**Reveals:** whether retention exists.

For each mechanism, ask what a user would actually lose by stopping. Data, habit, a process their
colleagues now depend on, other people, money spent moving.

If the honest answer is "nothing much", there is no retention mechanism — however good the
product is.

**Hides:** high switching costs retain users who resent it. That shows up later as poor referral
and bad renewals.

---

# 6. Location in the Product

**Reveals:** retention mechanisms that are hopes.

Cover the mechanism column and read only the requirements. Does each requirement genuinely
create a cost to leaving?

A requirement that stores nothing, involves nobody else, and sits outside any routine retains
nobody.

**Hides:** a requirement can create a cost to leaving that the user avoids paying by never
starting.

---

# 7. The Closure Test

**Reveals:** funnels wearing loops.

Does the output become the next input? Follow the arrow and check whether the thing at the end is
the same kind of thing as the thing at the start.

Users producing something that attracts users closes. Spend producing users does not.

**Hides:** a loop can close and still be too slow or too leaky to matter. Cycle time and leak
decide whether closure is worth anything.

---

# 8. The Second Turn

**Reveals:** whether a loop actually compounds.

Assume the loop runs once. What makes the second turn bigger than the first?

If nothing does, it closes but does not amplify — which is a self-sustaining process rather than
growth, and worth knowing.

**Hides:** early turns are noisy. A loop that looks flat at small numbers can compound later, and
the honest position is uncertainty rather than either claim.

---

# 9. The Ten Conversations

**Reveals:** whether the plan is concrete.

Name ten people or ten specific profiles, how each is reached, and what each would say no to.

The exercise fails immediately if the plan is a market description, and the failure is more
useful than another paragraph of strategy.

**Hides:** ten people who would say yes do not establish a market. They establish that the
product can be sold ten times, which is exactly what should be established first.

---

# 10. The Objection

**Reveals:** what the pitch has to survive.

From `05-competition`'s status quo analysis: why would this person keep doing what they do now?

The answer is usually not a competitor. It is that the current way works well enough and change
has a cost, which is the argument the plan actually has to win.

**Hides:** stated objections are not always real ones. The first no is often a proxy for
something harder to say.

---

# 11. Reading It as the Investor

**Reveals:** where the plan implies more than it establishes.

Read the document as someone deciding whether to fund it. Which numbers would they treat as
established? Which of those are actually assumptions?

Every gap between the two is growth laundering, and this lens finds it because the investor's
reading is the one the document was unconsciously written for.

**Hides:** nothing. It is the most uncomfortable lens in the module and the most useful.

---

# 12. The Spend Before the Answer

**Reveals:** money committed ahead of evidence.

Walk the sequence and mark where money is spent. Then check what is known at that point.

Where the problem is still assumed, acquisition spend amplifies uncertainty rather than resolving
it — and the resolution costs less than the campaign.

**Hides:** some spend genuinely is the experiment. A paid test designed to answer a question is
not the same as scaling a channel.

---

# Combining Models

| Situation | Model |
| --- | --- |
| Checking the motion is affordable | The Payback Arithmetic |
| Checking channels | Where Were They Standing? |
| Choosing the first channel | The Cost of Finding Out |
| Checking activation | The User Who Never Came Back |
| Checking retention exists | The Cost of Leaving |
| Checking retention is real | Location in the Product |
| Checking compounding | The Closure Test |
| Checking compounding matters | The Second Turn |
| Checking concreteness | The Ten Conversations |
| Checking the pitch | The Objection |
| Checking honesty | Reading It as the Investor |
| Checking sequence | The Spend Before the Answer |

Apply the Payback Arithmetic first — it can invalidate everything after it. Apply Reading It as
the Investor last, on the finished document.

---

# Self Assessment

- Does the arithmetic permit this motion?
- Where was each segment actually standing?
- Which channel is cheapest to learn from?
- Would my activation event count someone who never returned?
- What does a user lose by leaving?
- Does each retention requirement create that cost?
- Does anything close?
- What makes the second turn bigger?
- Can I name ten people?
- What is the real objection?
- Which of my numbers would an investor read as established?
- Where does money get spent before anything is known?

---

> **Mental Model Principle**
>
> Reading it as the investor is the lens that matters most, because
> that reader will not distinguish assumptions from findings.
>
> If the document does not do it for them, it will not get done.
