# Common Mistakes

---

# Overview

Growth plans fail expensively, because failure is measured in money spent rather than in
time. Most of the mistakes below are only detectable before the spending starts.

---

# 1. Borrowed Growth

**What it looks like.** A funnel model built on published conversion rates and channel
benchmarks.

**Why it is tempting.** The numbers exist, they are citable, and there is no alternative
source before launch.

**What it costs.** The benchmarks were measured in a market with a different switching
cost, buyer and alternative. Applied here they produce arithmetic that passes on figures
about somebody else's product — and the payback check, which is supposed to catch an
unaffordable plan, validates it instead.

**Instead.** Tag every borrowed figure `[assumption]`, name which one the plan is
standing on, and design the first spend as a measurement of that figure rather than as
an acquisition campaign.

---

# 2. The Funnel Drawn as a Loop

**What it looks like.** A circular diagram with an arrow from "happy users" back to "new
users."

**Why it is tempting.** Loops compound and funnels do not, so a loop is a much better
story. Word of mouth genuinely exists, which makes the arrow feel earned.

**What it costs.** A plan that assumes compounding it will not get. Spending is sized on
the assumption that each cohort seeds the next, and when it does not, the deficit appears
as a growth shortfall rather than as a modeling error.

**Instead.** Apply the closure test. If the return arrow depends on users choosing to do
promotional work, it is a funnel. Label it and plan accordingly — the worked example does
exactly this and is a better plan for it.

---

# 3. The Channel Named as a Category

**What it looks like.** "Content marketing, SEO, partnerships."

**Why it is tempting.** These are the channels that exist, and listing them looks like a
plan.

**What it costs.** None of them is a claim about where anyone is. The plan cannot be
wrong, so it cannot be corrected — and the first quarter is spent finding out what
should have been asked in an afternoon.

**Instead.** Name the place and the person. "The three regional practice-manager
associations, whose newsletters are read by the payer" is a channel. "Content marketing"
is a category.

---

# 4. The Payer Absent From the Channel Plan

**What it looks like.** Every channel reaches practitioners.

**Why it is tempting.** Practitioners are reachable, vocal and enthusiastic. The payer is
none of those.

**What it costs.** Demand that cannot convert. Enthusiasm accumulates at a level of the
organization that cannot buy, and the resulting pipeline stalls at exactly the same point
every time.

**Instead.** For each channel, say whether it reaches the user, the payer, or both.
Module 06 made the distinction; this is where it is spent.

---

# 5. Time to First Value Stated as an Adjective

**What it looks like.** "Users see value immediately."

**Why it is tempting.** It is the intent, and measuring it requires an actual flow.

**What it costs.** Nothing can be designed against it. The real interval — account
creation, an empty screen, data entry, a wait for processing — goes unmeasured, and the
drop-off is diagnosed as a channel quality problem.

**Instead.** Walk module 10's flow with a stopwatch. The number is usually much larger
than anyone expects, and it is nearly always fixable in the product rather than in the
funnel.

---

# 6. Retention Assumed From Usefulness

**What it looks like.** "Retention will be strong because the product solves a real
problem."

**Why it is tempting.** It follows from everything the run has established.

**What it costs.** Usefulness is necessary and insufficient. People leave useful products
constantly — a reorganization, a champion departing, a quiet lapse back into the old
workflow. A retention *mechanism* is structural: accumulated data, a dependency, a record
that has to live somewhere.

**Instead.** Name what makes leaving costly. If nothing does, that is a product finding
and it belongs on the roadmap.

---

# 7. Spending Before Milestone Zero

**What it looks like.** An acquisition budget scheduled alongside the first release, in a
run where module 04 declared a shortfall.

**Why it is tempting.** Launch and acquisition are natural companions, and holding back
feels timid.

**What it costs.** Money spent acquiring users for a problem nobody verified. If the
validation then disconfirms, the spending bought nothing except a set of customers for
the wrong product — and the framework's four-link chain, which existed precisely to
prevent this, ends one link short.

**Instead.** Gate it. The chain is 04 declares, 07 binds, 10 sequences, 11 withholds, and
this is the last link.

---

# 8. Optimizing the Funnel Instead of Fixing the Product

**What it looks like.** A plan whose response to poor conversion is to improve the landing
page and the email sequence.

**Why it is tempting.** Those are the levers this module owns, and they are quick.

**What it costs.** The most common cause of poor conversion is the switching cost module
03 measured, and no landing page reduces it. Effort goes into the surface while the
actual barrier is untouched.

**Instead.** Check the switching cost first. If it is high, the growth answer is usually a
product answer — a migration path, a parallel-running mode, a narrower entry point.

---

# The Pattern

| Mistake | Underlying move |
| --- | --- |
| Borrowed growth | Using available numbers instead of tagged ones |
| Funnel as loop | Preferring the compounding story |
| Channel as category | Listing what exists rather than where people are |
| Payer absent | Reaching who is reachable |
| Value time as adjective | Avoiding a measurement |
| Retention assumed | Concluding from usefulness |
| Spending before validation | Launch momentum overriding a gate |
| Optimizing the funnel | Working the lever you own |

Six of the eight substitute something available for something true. That is the
characteristic failure of growth planning, and it persists because the substitute always
produces a plan and the truth sometimes produces a delay.

---

> **Mistakes Principle**
>
> Every mistake here is discovered by spending money.
>
> That is what makes the pre-spend checks — closure, payback, and a real number for time
> to first value — worth more in this module than anywhere else in the framework.
