# Reflection

---

# Overview

A delivery plan encodes what you think progress is. These questions are about that.

---

# On the Sequence

**What did you schedule first, and why?** The honest answer is often "the part we know
how to build." That is comfortable and it defers the risk.

**What is the riskiest thing in this plan?** If it is not in the first third, ask what
would change if it were.

**What are you hoping to have learned by milestone two?** If nothing, the plan is a
construction schedule rather than a delivery plan.

---

# On the Flows

**Where does your flow start?** If it starts at a screen, you drew it from the product's
point of view.

**Which state did you design last?** It is nearly always the empty one, which is the one
everyone sees first.

**What does the user do when it fails?** Not what the system does. What the person does,
at that moment, to get their work finished. In the worked example the answer was that a
manual path stays available — and that turned out to be a requirement rather than a
courtesy.

---

# On the Handoff

**Who could build from this without you?** Name someone. If you cannot, it is a summary
of conversations.

**What did you leave out because it was obvious?** Everything you just thought of belongs
in the document.

**What decisions did you record the reasoning for?** A builder who does not know why will
re-decide, reasonably, and differently.

---

# On the Milestones

**Which one could you demonstrate tomorrow if you had to?** If none, the first
demonstration is a long way off and so is the first real information.

**Which one contains the integration risk?** Concentrated integration risk at the end is
the standard shape and the standard cause of overrun.

**How would you know a milestone was late?** If the answer is "when someone says so,"
there is no signal in the plan.

---

# On Milestone Zero

**Did the run carry a shortfall, and is it first?** Check rather than assume. This is the
last place in the framework where that chain can still be honored, and it is the easiest
place to quietly drop it, because building is more appealing than validating.

---

# On Your Own Pattern

| Look for | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| You always start with the part you know | Risk is being deferred, not managed |
| Your milestones are layers | You plan by technical decomposition |
| Your empty states are designed last | You design the version worth showing |
| Your handoffs generate the same questions each project | Those are the decisions you habitually keep |
| Validation is always after the first release | Progress feels like building, to you |

---

# The Question Worth Returning To

> If the first milestone shipped and nothing else ever did, would anyone be better off?

A plan whose first milestone leaves the user with nothing usable has sequenced by
convenience. This question is not an argument for shipping fragments — it is a test of
whether the sequence was built around value or around dependency order.

The two coincide less often than plans assume.

---

> **Reflection Principle**
>
> A delivery plan is a statement about what you believe progress is.
>
> Layers feel like progress and produce none until the end. Slices feel slower and
> produce information from the second week.
