# Evaluation

---

# Overview

One question runs through all of these: **point at it.** For every claim, where is the
thing you observed?

---

# Exercise 1 — Audit Your Own Tags

Take any problem list you have written. For each line, write where the evidence came
from in one clause. Then re-tag.

**Passing looks like.** Some lines move from `[verified]` to `[inferred]`. In a first
pass this usually happens to a third of them.

**Failing looks like.** Nothing moves. Either the list was unusually well sourced, or
the audit was performed by the same instinct that wrote it.

---

# Exercise 2 — Find the Workaround

Pick a job you know well. Find three workarounds people have built for it — a
spreadsheet, a habit, a convention, a person who is asked.

**Passing looks like.** You can say what each workaround implies about the requirement.
A spreadsheet with a manual reconciliation column is a specification.

**Failing looks like.** You cannot find one. That is a finding: either you do not know
the job well enough, or the problem is not frequent enough to have produced a response.

---

# Exercise 3 — Rank Against Your Own Preference

Score four problems on all three axes without looking at the totals. Then reveal them.

**Passing looks like.** The winner is not the one you wanted to solve. This happens
roughly half the time when the scoring is done honestly.

**Failing looks like.** The winner is always the interesting one. That is the
reverse-engineered ranking, and it is undetectable in the output.

---

# Exercise 4 — Two More Whys

Take a problem statement and ask why twice more.

**Passing looks like.** You end up somewhere organizational, or at an incentive.

**Failing looks like.** You end up at "because the software does not do it." That is a
loop back to the symptom, and it means the first why was answered with a solution.

---

# Exercise 5 — Write a Plan That Could Fail

For your sharpest problem, write a validation step and state the specific result that
would disconfirm it.

**Passing looks like.** A disconfirming result you would find genuinely unwelcome.

**Failing looks like.** "If users say they don't have this problem." That is not a
threshold; it is a restatement. A threshold has a number or an observable.

---

# Exercise 6 — Practice the Declaration

Write a `declared_shortfall` for a real idea of yours: what is missing, why, what would
fill it, and what module 07 should do differently as a result.

**Passing looks like.** The last part is specific — a weighting change, a preference for
approaches that survive the problem being wrong, a threshold before spending.

**Failing looks like.** The declaration ends at "more research needed." That version
travels nowhere, and a shortfall that changes nothing downstream is decoration.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Produces a problem list; cannot say which items are inferred |
| **Working** | Tags honestly on review; still ranks by severity alone |
| **Competent** | Scores all three axes with evidence attached; reads workarounds as specifications |
| **Fluent** | Declares a shortfall without discomfort and states its downstream consequence; writes validation plans that can fail |

---

# A Note on What Cannot Be Assessed Here

Whether a well-evidenced problem is worth building a business around. A problem can be
frequent, severe, poorly served, and still support no business — because the people who
have it cannot pay, or are unreachable, or are too few.

Modules 05 and 06 answer that. The most common misuse of a strong module 04 output is
treating it as a decision to proceed, and the confidence a good evidence log produces
is exactly what makes that misuse feel justified.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> For every problem on your list, someone should be able to ask "how do you know" and
> get a specific answer.
>
> Where the honest answer is "I don't," that is not a failed exercise. It is the
> module working.
