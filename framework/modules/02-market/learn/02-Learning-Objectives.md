# Learning Objectives

---

# Overview

> After this module you should be able to define a market someone could disagree with,
> size it in a way that survives one of its inputs being wrong, and name what is
> legally true about it.

Each clause is a separate skill and they fail independently.

---

# Knowledge Objectives

You should understand:

- The difference between a market **boundary** and a market **description**
- What TAM, SAM and SOM each mean, and which decisions each one informs
- Why the three figures are derived rather than looked up
- What a **trend** is, as distinct from an observation — a trend has a direction
- Why timing has a state, and what makes a market EARLY, RIGHT or LATE
- That a regulatory finding becomes an obligation with a schedule, not a note

---

# Thinking Objectives

The shift this module asks for is from *how big* to *where does it end*.

Instead of asking:

> "Is this a big market?"

ask:

- What is inside this boundary and what is just outside it?
- Which of my four sizing inputs is the one I am least sure of?
- If that input is wrong by a factor of three, does anything change?
- What changed recently that makes this possible now?
- What would have to change for this to become possible later?
- Which jurisdiction am I actually operating in, and who says so?

---

# Skill Objectives

You should be able to:

- Write a market definition that excludes something
- Build a sizing chain from named inputs, each labeled with its evidence status
- Run a sensitivity on the weakest input rather than on all of them
- Distinguish a trend with a direction and a source from an industry sentiment
- Give a timing verdict and defend it with something that changed
- Identify the applicable regulatory regime by naming the jurisdiction first

---

# Analytical Objectives

You should develop the ability to:

- Notice when a market figure has a hidden step
- Recognize a top-down number dressed as a bottom-up one
- Tell a market gap from a feature nobody has built because nobody wants it
- Separate what the market is from what the market will be, and label the second

---

# Judgment Objectives

Two judgments in this module have no mechanical answer:

**Where to draw the boundary.** Too wide and nothing downstream is checkable; too
narrow and the sizing is trivially small. The test is whether the boundary makes
membership decidable — not whether the resulting number is comfortable.

**Whether the timing claim is real.** Every idea can be given a "why now." The
objective is to distinguish a "why now" resting on something that actually changed
from one resting on a general sense that the moment has arrived.

---

# You Have Learned This When

| Signal | What it indicates |
| --- | --- |
| Your first question about a market is where it stops | The boundary habit has formed |
| You state the weakest input before anyone asks | Sizing is an argument to you, not a result |
| You can name the jurisdiction without hedging | The regulatory finding is real work, not a caveat |
| A LATE timing verdict does not feel like a defeat | Timing is information rather than judgment |
| You are comfortable with a small SOM | You are sizing what you can reach, not what you hope for |

The last one is the hardest, and it is the one that most changes the quality of
everything downstream.

---

# What This Module Does Not Teach You

It does not teach competitive analysis — that is module 05 and it needs this module's
boundary first. It does not teach pricing. It does not tell you whether to proceed.

A learner who finishes this module knowing exactly how big the market is and nothing
about where it ends has learned the least useful half.

---

> **Objectives Principle**
>
> The skill is not producing a market number. It is producing one that can be
> corrected.
>
> Everything else here follows from taking that seriously.
