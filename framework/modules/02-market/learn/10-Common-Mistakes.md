# Common Mistakes

---

# Overview

Market work has a distinctive failure mode: the output looks more authoritative than
any other module's, because it contains numbers. The mistakes below all survive review
for that reason.

---

# 1. The Top-Down Number Wearing Bottom-Up Clothes

**What it looks like.** "The global market is $40bn. We need 0.1% of it."

**Why it is tempting.** It produces a large, citable figure in one line, and the 0.1%
sounds modest.

**What it costs.** The percentage is not a plan — it is a wish with a decimal point.
Module 06 later needs a path to the first ten customers, and a top-down figure
contains no information about where a single one of them comes from.

**Instead.** Count upward. How many of the thing exist, what fraction is reachable,
what would each pay, how often. Four inputs you can argue about beat one you cannot.

---

# 2. The Market Defined So It Cannot Exclude Anything

**What it looks like.** "The productivity software market."

**Why it is tempting.** A bigger boundary produces a bigger number, and narrowing feels
like conceding.

**What it costs.** Module 05's gate requires competitors, and with no boundary the
competitor set is either everything or arbitrary. The gap analysis that follows is
then a gap in a set nobody defined.

**Instead.** State something that is outside. A definition that excludes nothing has
defined nothing.

---

# 3. Trends Without Direction

**What it looks like.** "AI adoption is a significant trend in this space."

**Why it is tempting.** It is true, and it is what everyone else writes.

**What it costs.** A trend without a direction and a source cannot be used. Module 07
needs to know whether a condition is improving or deteriorating in order to sequence
against it, and "significant" carries no vector.

**Instead.** Direction, magnitude if available, source, date. "Practice consolidation
reduced single-handed practices by n% over five years [source, date]" is a trend.
The other thing is a topic.

---

# 4. The Timing Verdict Everyone Gives

**What it looks like.** Every idea, on inspection, turns out to be arriving at exactly
the right moment.

**Why it is tempting.** The alternative verdicts are unwelcome, and there is always a
recent development that can be pointed at.

**What it costs.** A timing verdict that is always RIGHT carries no information, so
module 07 cannot weight anything by it. Worse, an EARLY market misread as RIGHT
produces a growth plan whose channels do not exist yet.

**Instead.** Name what changed and when. If nothing changed, the honest verdict is
that the timing is unchanged — which usually means LATE, and LATE is a workable
finding. It generally means the buyer already understands the problem.

---

# 5. The Regulatory Line That Says "Consider the Regulatory Context"

**What it looks like.** A sentence acknowledging that regulation exists.

**Why it is tempting.** Naming the actual regime requires finding it, and finding it
sometimes produces an obligation you would rather not have.

**What it costs.** This is the most expensive mistake in the module, because it breaks
the obligation chain at its origin. Nothing in module 09 gets built, nothing in module
13 gets scheduled, and the gap is invisible until it is a legal event.

**Instead.** Name the jurisdiction, the regime, and the specific obligation, with its
citation and the date you checked. If it is unresolved, it is a blocker — record it as
one rather than as a risk.

---

# 6. The Gap That Is Empty for a Reason

**What it looks like.** "No competitor offers X. That is our opening."

**Why it is tempting.** An empty space looks like an opportunity by default.

**What it costs.** Module 05 will test the gap and module 07 will build strategy on it.
A gap that exists because the thing is unwanted, unbuildable, or illegal produces a
strategy aimed at a hole.

**Instead.** For every gap, ask why it is empty. Three answers are possible — nobody
has got to it, nobody can, or nobody should. Only the first is an opening, and it is
the least common.

---

# 7. Sizing Every Input Instead of the Weak One

**What it looks like.** A sensitivity table varying all four inputs by ±20%.

**Why it is tempting.** It looks thorough.

**What it costs.** It hides the actual exposure. One input is usually far less certain
than the others, and varying it by 20% when it could be wrong by 300% understates the
risk while appearing to have addressed it.

**Instead.** Name the load-bearing input and vary it across its real plausible range.
One honest sensitivity beats four decorative ones.

---

# The Pattern

| Mistake | Underlying move |
| --- | --- |
| Top-down percentage | Producing a number without a mechanism |
| Unbounded market | Avoiding exclusion |
| Trend without direction | Reporting a topic as a finding |
| Timing always RIGHT | A verdict that cannot be negative |
| Regulation acknowledged | Avoiding an obligation by not looking |
| Gap assumed to be an opening | Treating absence as evidence |
| Uniform sensitivity | Thoroughness as cover |

Four of the seven produce a document that is more confident than the evidence supports.
That is this module's characteristic failure, and it is worth watching for
specifically, because numbers make it harder to see.

---

> **Mistakes Principle**
>
> The market section is the most-believed page in most product documents and the
> least-checked.
>
> Every mistake here survives because it arrives with a number attached.
