# Evaluation

---

# Overview

Market work is easy to assess because it makes falsifiable claims. Each exercise below
produces something someone else could disagree with — which is the property being
tested.

---

# Exercise 1 — Draw a Boundary That Excludes

Pick any product. Write its market definition in one sentence, then name three
organizations that are **just outside** the boundary and say why.

**Passing looks like.** The three are genuinely close — plausible members that the
definition excludes for a stated reason.

**Failing looks like.** The three are obviously unrelated. If you had to reach for
something absurd to find an exclusion, the boundary is not doing work.

---

# Exercise 2 — Build a Chain, Then Break It

Size any market bottom-up in four steps. Then identify which single input, if wrong by
a factor of three, changes the conclusion.

**Passing looks like.** You can name that input immediately, and it is not the one you
sourced most carefully. The weak link is almost always the one you estimated.

**Failing looks like.** Every input feels equally certain. That is a sign the chain was
built to reach a number rather than to be examined.

---

# Exercise 3 — Convert a Topic Into a Trend

Take a sentence like "remote work is growing in this sector" and rewrite it with a
direction, a magnitude, a source and a date.

**Passing looks like.** You either produce the full version or you discover you cannot
source it — and mark it `[assumption]`.

**Failing looks like.** You produce the full version with plausible-sounding figures
you did not verify. This is the failure the evidence policy is written against, and it
is easiest to commit here because trend statements are so quotable.

---

# Exercise 4 — Argue the Timing Both Ways

For an idea you like, write the strongest case that the timing is RIGHT and the
strongest case that it is LATE. Both in earnest.

**Passing looks like.** The LATE case is genuinely uncomfortable to read.

**Failing looks like.** The LATE case is a straw man. If you cannot make it, you do not
yet know the market well enough to claim the timing.

---

# Exercise 5 — Find the Jurisdiction

Take a product handling any sensitive data. Name the country, the regime, one specific
obligation it creates, and where you found it.

**Passing looks like.** A citation you could hand to someone else.

**Failing looks like.** A regime named from memory. Regulations change; a remembered
obligation is an assumption wearing the clothes of a fact.

---

# Exercise 6 — Explain an Empty Gap

Find a feature no competitor in some market offers. Write the three candidate reasons —
nobody has, nobody can, nobody should — and decide which applies.

**Passing looks like.** You conclude "nobody should" at least once across a few
attempts. Most gaps are that.

**Failing looks like.** Every gap turns out to be an opportunity.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Produces a market size; cannot say what is outside the market |
| **Working** | Builds a chain, but treats all inputs as equally solid |
| **Competent** | Names the load-bearing input unprompted; states the jurisdiction with a citation |
| **Fluent** | Gives an uncomfortable timing verdict, and treats a small defensible SOM as a better result than a large indefensible one |

---

# A Note on What Cannot Be Assessed Here

Whether the market is *worth entering*. No exercise on this page tests that, because
this module does not answer it. The judgment is assembled from modules 05, 06 and 07
together, and treating a large TAM as the answer is the single most common way a
research process arrives at a confident wrong conclusion.

A market can be large, growing, and a terrible place to build a business. Module 02
will tell you the first two and is silent on the third by design.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> A market claim you cannot argue against is not a strong claim. It is an unfalsifiable
> one.
>
> Every exercise here tests the same property: whether someone could tell you that you
> are wrong.
