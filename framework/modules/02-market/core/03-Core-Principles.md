# Core Principles

---

# Overview

These principles govern judgment in this module — what to do when the workflow does not
cover the situation in front of you.

---

# Principle Statement

> A market is not a number. It is a boundary, a population inside it, and the rules
> that decide who may serve them.

---

# Principle 1 — Boundary Before Number

Draw the boundary first. Always.

A number produced before the boundary is a number attached to nothing. It will look
precise, travel through four modules, and mislead all of them.

If the boundary cannot be drawn, that is the finding — not a reason to reach for a
figure that can be.

---

# Principle 2 — Show the Arithmetic

Never present a figure alone.

A derivation invites disagreement, and disagreement is how errors surface. A bare number
can only be believed or disbelieved, which means it can only be wrong silently.

> If a reader cannot argue with your figure, you have not given them evidence.
> You have given them an assertion.

---

# Principle 3 — Regulation Before Sizing

Establish who may legally participate before counting who exists.

A market of 100,000 where only certified entities may sell is not a market of 100,000.
Sizing first and regulating second produces a figure that is wrong by an order of
magnitude and looks entirely reasonable.

---

# Principle 4 — Someone Else's Number Measured Someone Else's Market

An external market size was produced inside a boundary somebody else drew, for reasons
you did not see.

Before adopting one, compare its boundary to yours. They usually differ. When they do,
the figure is measuring a different market — and using it silently imports that
difference into everything downstream.

---

# Principle 5 — Every Market Pushes Both Ways

There is no market where all forces favor a new entrant.

If the research found only favorable trends, the research was shaped by the idea rather
than by the market. Search deliberately for what argues against.

The unfavorable finding is more valuable than the favorable one, because nobody else in
the process is looking for it.

---

# Principle 6 — A Gap With No Reason Is Not a Gap

When a market appears to leave an obvious need unserved, there is almost always a reason:
regulation, economics, distribution, data access, or misaligned incentives.

Finding the reason tells you whether the gap is an opportunity or a trap.

Finding no reason usually means the search stopped early — not that a free opportunity
has been discovered.

---

# Principle 7 — The Container, Not the Contents

This module establishes where the market starts and stops, how large it is, and what
governs it.

It does not name competitors, build personas, or set prices. Those modules will have
context this one does not, and will do the work better.

Work done outside this module's scope has to be redone, and meanwhile it hides the fact
that the container was never properly described.

---

# Principle 8 — Write for the Reader Seven Modules Away

The regulatory findings here are consumed by `09-technology`, long after this document
has stopped being read.

"HIPAA applies" is a label. The obligations it implies — encryption, audit retention
periods, breach notification windows, subprocessor agreements — are the specification.
Write the specification.

---

# Principle 9 — Uncertainty Is Reportable

A market that cannot be sized from available sources is a legitimate finding.

State it, size it bottom-up from stated assumptions, tag every input honestly, and lower
the confidence level.

An analysis that admits what it could not establish is usable. One that invents a citation
to fill the gap destroys trust in every other claim it makes.

---

# Principle 10 — Timing Is a Finding

Markets are not only large or small. They are early, on time, or late.

Being early is a distribution and education problem. Being late is a differentiation
problem. Both are survivable when named, and neither is when it is not.

---

# Principle Hierarchy

Evaluate a market in this order:

```
Boundary
   ↓
Regulation
   ↓
Size
   ↓
Direction
   ↓
Gap
   ↓
Structure
```

Each level is meaningless without the one above it.

---

# Common Violations

- Reaching for a market size before defining the market.
- Adopting an analyst figure without checking its boundary.
- Sizing a population that regulation excludes from being served.
- Reporting only trends that support the idea.
- Asserting a gap with no explanation for its persistence.
- Building a competitor comparison inside this module.
- Writing regulatory findings as acronyms rather than obligations.
- Filling an evidence gap with a plausible-sounding source.

---

# Self Assessment

- Did I draw the boundary before reaching for a number?
- Can a reader argue with every figure I produced?
- Did I establish regulation before sizing?
- Did I check the boundary of any figure I borrowed?
- Did I find what argues against this market?
- Does every gap explain itself?
- Have I stayed inside this module's scope?

---

> **Core Principle**
>
> A market analysis is not an argument for the idea.
>
> It is an honest description of the ground the idea would have to stand on.
