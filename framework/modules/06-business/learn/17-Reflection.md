# Reflection

---

# Overview

You built a model that concluded something. These questions are about how it got there.

---

# On the Conclusion

**Did you know the answer before you built it?** Most people do. The useful question is
what happened when an input disagreed.

**Which input did you revise last?** That one was doing the reconciling. Note whether
you revised it because you found better information.

**How would you know if this were wrong?** Write the specific signal. "Slower growth
than expected" is not one. "Fewer than n of the first fifty trials convert" is.

---

# On the Price

**Did you compute the value, or did you look at competitors?** Both is correct. One is
the common shortcut.

**Would you pay it?** Answer as the payer, not as the user. They have different budgets
and different alternatives.

**What is the highest price you considered, and why did you reject it?** Frequently the
reason is discomfort rather than evidence. Underpricing is far more common than
overpricing in first models, and it is the harder mistake to reverse afterward.

---

# On the Costs

**What did you not put in the model?** There is always something. Support hours,
onboarding, the payment processor's cut, the free tier's infrastructure, your own time.

**Whose time is free in your model?** Usually yours. Ask what the model looks like when
it is not.

**What happens at ten times the volume?** Some costs are flat, some scale linearly, one
usually scales worse than expected. Knowing which is which is what modules 09 and 13
will come back and ask about.

---

# On the Assumptions

**Which one is the model standing on?** If you cannot answer immediately, the model is
not yet understood — only assembled.

**Where did each number come from?** Trace them. In most first models at least one
central figure has no origin anybody can recall.

**Which number did you choose because it made the model work?** This is the
uncomfortable one and it is worth answering honestly, because it identifies exactly
where the model will break.

---

# On Your Own Pattern

| Look for | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Your models always close | Inputs are being selected rather than found |
| You price below the value ceiling every time | You are pricing against your own comfort |
| Your costs are infrastructure only | Human time is invisible to you |
| You use LTV/CAC | You are building decisions on an unknowable input |
| Your first customers are a channel | The reachability question is being deferred |

---

# The Question Worth Returning To

> If module 13 comes back and says the true cost to serve exceeds your ceiling, what
> will you actually do?

Answer it now. After the fact, the answer negotiates itself into "the cost will come
down as we scale," which is the failure the three arithmetic checks were built to
catch.

Deciding in advance — raise the price, cut the scope, or stop — is the difference
between a check that works and a check that is absorbed.

---

> **Reflection Principle**
>
> Business models are the easiest place in the framework to be precisely wrong.
>
> The arithmetic will always be correct. The reflection that matters is about which
> numbers went into it, and who chose them.
