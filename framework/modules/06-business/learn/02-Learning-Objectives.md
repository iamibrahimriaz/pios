# Learning Objectives

---

# Overview

> After this module you should be able to say who pays and why, defend a price against
> both a value calculation and a competitor, and name the single assumption your model
> is standing on.

---

# Knowledge Objectives

You should understand:

- The difference between the user, the payer, and the person who can veto
- How value-based and competition-constrained pricing differ, and why you need both
- What the common pricing structures do to behavior — per seat, usage, tiered, flat,
  freemium — and which failure each is prone to
- What CAC, LTV, payback period and churn mean, and why none is knowable pre-launch
- Why a payback ceiling is more useful pre-launch than an LTV/CAC ratio
- What a cost to serve includes, including the operator's own hours

---

# Thinking Objectives

The shift is from *can we make money* to *what would have to be true*.

Instead of asking:

> "Is this a good business?"

ask:

- Who signs, and what are they comparing this against?
- What does the alternative cost them, in money and in hours?
- Which of my four inputs is the one I am least able to source?
- How wrong can it be before the answer changes?
- What is the ceiling this sets for everything downstream?

---

# Skill Objectives

You should be able to:

- Identify the payer, the user and the veto, and say when they are the same person
- Compute a value ceiling from the alternative's real cost
- Capture the competitive constraint from module 05 without softening it
- Choose a pricing structure and state the behavior it produces
- Build unit economics with every input tagged
- Run one honest sensitivity on the load-bearing input
- Describe a concrete path to the first ten customers

---

# Analytical Objectives

You should develop the ability to:

- Notice when a model's conclusion is insensitive to every input — which means it was
  constructed, not derived
- Recognize a benchmark borrowed from a different switching-cost environment
- Tell a pricing structure that matches value delivery from one that matches billing
  convenience
- Spot a cost model that omits the founder's own time

---

# Judgment Objectives

**Where to set the price.** Between the value ceiling and the competitive constraint,
there is usually a range rather than a point. The judgment should be recorded with its
reasoning, because module 13 may later come back and ask for it to move.

**Which structure fits.** Per-seat pricing on a product used by one person in an
organization produces a small contract regardless of value. Usage pricing on an
unpredictable workload produces a purchase nobody can approve. The structure decides
more than the number does.

---

# You Have Learned This When

| Signal | What it indicates |
| --- | --- |
| Your first question is who signs | The payer habit has formed |
| You price the alternative before pricing the product | Value is computed, not asserted |
| You can name your load-bearing assumption without looking | The model is understood rather than assembled |
| A failed cost check makes you revisit the price rather than the cost | The regress rule is internalized |
| You include your own hours in the cost to serve | The most commonly omitted cost is present |

---

# What This Module Does Not Teach You

It does not teach acquisition tactics — module 11 does, against this module's ceiling.
It does not choose what to build. It does not tell you the business is viable; it tells
you what would have to be true, and three later modules test it.

---

> **Objectives Principle**
>
> The skill is knowing which number your business is standing on.
>
> Everything else in this module is arithmetic, and arithmetic is the easy part.
