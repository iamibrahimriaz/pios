# Evaluation

---

# Overview

The test underneath these: **can your model produce a negative answer?** A model that
cannot has not been built; it has been arranged.

---

# Exercise 1 — Name Three People

For a product sold into organizations, name who uses it, who signs, and who can veto.
Then say what each one would ask first.

**Passing looks like.** Three different first questions. The veto's question is usually
about risk or about something the product team never considered.

**Failing looks like.** One person and one question. Occasionally correct; usually
means the other two were not looked for.

---

# Exercise 2 — Compute a Value Ceiling

Take a task a product removes. Price it: hours per month, loaded hourly cost, monthly
value. Then find what competitors charge.

**Passing looks like.** Two numbers that differ, and a stated reason for choosing where
between them to sit.

**Failing looks like.** One number. If you only produced the competitor price, you
skipped the calculation that tells you whether the price is a bargain or a stretch.

---

# Exercise 3 — Break Your Own Model

Build unit economics for any product. Then find the input that, moved within its
genuinely plausible range, flips the conclusion.

**Passing looks like.** You find one quickly. Almost every pre-launch model has one,
usually CAC or churn.

**Failing looks like.** Nothing flips it. That means the ranges you allowed were too
narrow — a 20% band on a number that could be wrong by a factor of four is not a range.

---

# Exercise 4 — Price Your Own Time

Take a side project or a service you have delivered. Compute the cost to serve one
customer including your own hours at a real rate.

**Passing looks like.** The total is substantially larger than you expected, and the
human time dominates.

**Failing looks like.** Infrastructure dominates. For most small products it does not,
and a model saying otherwise has priced hours at zero.

---

# Exercise 5 — Replace LTV With a Payback Ceiling

Take a model that uses an LTV/CAC ratio. Rewrite the spending rule as: what a customer
returns in n months at the stated price and gross margin.

**Passing looks like.** The permitted spend drops, sometimes sharply.

**Failing looks like.** It stays the same. Then the original churn assumption was
already conservative, which is worth knowing and is unusual.

---

# Exercise 6 — Name the First Three

For any product idea, name three specific people or organizations who would be among
the first ten customers, and why each would take the meeting.

**Passing looks like.** Three names and three reasons.

**Failing looks like.** A channel. If no names exist, the reachability question has not
been answered and module 11 will inherit the gap.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Produces a model; every input feels equally solid |
| **Working** | Tags assumptions; still uses borrowed benchmarks as load-bearing inputs |
| **Competent** | Value and constraint both computed; load-bearing assumption named; own time priced |
| **Fluent** | Treats a failed downstream cost check as a reason to revisit price or scope rather than the forecast, and can name the first three customers |

---

# A Note on What Cannot Be Assessed Here

Whether the business will work. Nothing on this page tests that, and nothing in this
module does either. What the module produces is a set of conditions — a price, a cost
ceiling, a payback limit — that modules 09, 11 and 13 will each test a different part
of.

The most common misuse of a completed business model is to treat it as the decision. It
is the specification of what the decision depends on.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> A model you cannot break is not conservative. It is unconnected to anything that
> could vary.
>
> Every exercise here is an attempt to break one, because that is the only way to find
> out what it is standing on.
