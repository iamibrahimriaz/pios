# Core Principles

---

# Principle Statement

> You are describing a real person who will never read this document and cannot
> correct you.
>
> That obligation is the whole of this module's discipline.

---

# Principle 1 — One Person, Not a Category

"Healthcare professionals" is a category. Categories cannot be designed for, because every
design decision that suits one member harms another.

Narrow until you are describing someone specific enough that a design decision could be
wrong for them. That specificity is what makes the persona useful.

---

# Principle 2 — Behavior Divides, Description Does Not

A segment exists when its members would buy, use, or reject the product for different
reasons.

Age, region, and company size are descriptions. They become segments only when they
produce different behavior — and if they do, name the behavior, not the description.

---

# Principle 3 — Observe Before Interpreting

What people do is evidence. What they want is interpretation.

Interpret first and the interpretation will describe the product you already had in mind.
Document the workflow before naming the jobs, every time.

---

# Principle 4 — A Job Outlives Its Solution

A job is what the person is trying to achieve, stated so that it remains true however the
product is built.

If the sentence names a tool, a feature, or a technology, it is a solution wearing a job's
grammar — and it locks in the answer before the problem has been examined.

---

# Principle 5 — The Real Competitor Is What They Do Today

Usually it is not a product. It is paper, a spreadsheet, a habit, an assistant, or doing
nothing at all.

Those competitors are free, already installed, fully trusted, and require no change. They
are harder to displace than any funded rival, and they are invisible unless the current
workflow is documented properly.

---

# Principle 6 — Switching Cost Decides Adoption

A better product that costs two weeks of disruption loses to a worse product already in
place.

Migration, retraining, downtime, contracts and fear are not footnotes to adoption. They
are the mechanism of it. State the bar the product must clear, honestly.

---

# Principle 7 — Never Invent a Voice

A fabricated user quote is the most damaging thing this module can produce.

It reads as primary evidence. It propagates into the dossier, the PRD and the pitch. No
downstream reader can tell it was invented, because there is nothing to check it against.

If no real voice was found, write that no real voice was found.

---

# Principle 8 — Label Inference as Inference

An inferred persona is legitimate research when it says so.

The failure is not inferring. The failure is inference that arrives wearing the clothes of
observation — and every reader afterwards treating a guess as a finding.

---

# Principle 9 — What Is Not Known Is a Deliverable

The gaps section is not an admission of incomplete work. It is the validation list module
04 will use.

A persona with honest gaps is more useful than one that appears complete, because the
first tells the team what to check and the second tells them there is nothing to check.

---

# Principle 10 — The Immovable Defines the Possible

Every user has things they will not change, however good the product is: a habit, a tool,
a professional obligation, a physical constraint.

A product that requires an immovable to move does not get adopted. Finding those
constraints early shapes what can be built; finding them late explains why nobody used it.

---

# Principle 11 — Prioritize on Criteria, Not on Availability

The segment with the most published material is not the segment worth serving. It is the
segment that is easiest to research.

Score against pain, ability to pay, reachability and speed to first customer — then choose,
and state what the choice costs.

---

# Principle Hierarchy

```
Segment
   ↓
Priority
   ↓
Person
   ↓
Current Behavior
   ↓
Switching Cost
   ↓
Job
```

Each level depends on the one above. A job derived without observed behavior is a guess
with good grammar.

---

# Common Violations

- Segmenting by description instead of behavior.
- Choosing the segment that was easiest to find sources for.
- Writing a persona that could describe anyone.
- Inventing a quote, a name, or a statistic.
- Naming jobs before documenting behavior.
- Writing a job that contains the intended solution.
- Treating switching cost as an afterthought.
- Omitting the gaps section because it looks like weakness.
- Reporting `high` confidence with no primary research.

---

# Self Assessment

- Am I describing one person, or a category?
- Do my segments differ in behavior?
- Did I observe before I interpreted?
- Would my jobs survive a completely different product?
- Did I name what they use today, specifically?
- Did I assess what leaving it costs?
- Did I invent anything at all?
- Did I say what I do not know?

---

> **Core Principle**
>
> The user cannot correct what you write about them.
>
> Write as though they will read it, and mark clearly everything you had to guess.
