# Best Practices

---

# 1. Read the Three-Star Reviews First

Five-star reviews are enthusiasm. One-star reviews are often one bad support experience.

Three-star reviews come from people who use the product daily, have thought about it, and
will qualify their complaints precisely. They contain the workflow.

---

# 2. Mine Migration Threads

People describe in exhaustive detail what went wrong the last time they switched tools.

That is your switching cost section, written by the users themselves, with the parts they
found painful already emphasized.

Search for "migrating from", "switching from", "moved off", plus the incumbent's name.

---

# 3. Read the Competitor's Help Center

Every support article exists because enough people got stuck to justify writing it.

The table of contents is a ranked list of where the current workflow fails.

---

# 4. Use Job Postings as Workflow Evidence

A posting states which tools a role uses, which tasks consume time, and what problem the
organization is hiring to solve.

It is unusually honest evidence, because nobody writes a job posting to persuade a
product team.

---

# 5. Write the Persona Attribute by Attribute, Not as a Story

Narrative invites invention. A story wants to be coherent, and coherence fills gaps with
plausible detail.

Fill the table row by row, asking for each: what does a source actually say, and what am I
inferring? The prose version, if needed, comes after.

---

# 6. Name the Incumbent Specifically

"Their existing system" is not an answer. "Paper notes transcribed into «product» after
hours" is.

Module 05 needs the name. And the specificity usually reveals the real problem — the
after-hours transcription, not the software.

---

# 7. Count the Steps

A workflow described in prose hides its length. The same workflow as a numbered list
reveals that it takes eleven steps and four tools.

Step count is the most persuasive number this module produces, and the easiest to lose by
writing paragraphs.

---

# 8. Look for the Workaround

A widely shared workaround is a product gap with evidence attached — and someone has
already quantified how much it annoys people by how often it is shared.

Spreadsheet templates, browser extensions, printed cheat sheets, and "here's my process"
forum posts are all workarounds.

---

# 9. Ask Who Gets Blamed

In organizational contexts, the deciding question is rarely "is this better?" It is "who
is responsible if this goes wrong?"

That person is the blocker. They are almost never the user, almost never interviewed, and
frequently decide the outcome.

---

# 10. State the Research Mode in the First Ten Lines

A reader must know within seconds whether this document is built on interviews, on proxy
sources, or on reasoning alone.

Burying it in a footnote at the end means every reader who skims treats inference as
observation.

---

# 11. Prefer Behavioral Evidence Over Opinion

What people do beats what they say they want.

"14 reviewers describe exporting to a spreadsheet before their weekly meeting" is stronger
evidence than "reviewers say they want better reporting" — the first is a behavior, the
second is a wish.

---

# 12. Note Who Is Missing From Your Sources

Review writers are self-selected. Forum posters are technically engaged. The silent
majority — people for whom the current workflow is merely tolerable — leave no trace.

They are usually the largest group and the hardest to convert. Say that they are absent
from the evidence.

---

# 13. Write the Gaps Section Before You Feel Finished

The instinct is to write it last, apologetically, or to skip it.

Written early, it stops the temptation to fill gaps with plausible content — because the
gap now has a home to go to.

---

# 14. Keep a Running NEEDS USER List

Every time research hits something only a real person could answer, write it down rather
than working around it.

That list becomes the first validation tasks in module 04's plan, and it is one of the
most practically useful things this module hands forward.

---

# 15. Stop at the Problem Boundary

When ranking problems by severity starts to feel natural, stop. That is module 04.

This module establishes who they are and what they do. Module 04 establishes what hurts,
with the segment and workflow context this module provides.

---

# Anti-Practices

| Habit | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Writing the persona as a story | Invites invented detail |
| Inventing a quote to illustrate a point | Undetectable fabrication downstream |
| Segmenting by demographics | Produces two personas who are one person |
| Choosing the best-documented segment | Serves the researchable user, not the right one |
| Naming jobs before observing behavior | The job describes the intended product |
| Treating switching cost as a footnote | The product loses to an installed inferior tool |
| Omitting the gaps section | Hides what most needs validating |
| Claiming high confidence without primary research | Misrepresents the entire blueprint |

---

# Self Assessment

- Did I read the three-star reviews?
- Did I find a migration thread?
- Is my persona built row by row, or written as a story?
- Did I name the incumbent specifically?
- Did I count the steps?
- Do I know who would get blamed if this went wrong?
- Is my research mode visible in the first ten lines?
- Did I record who is missing from my sources?

---

> **Practice Principle**
>
> Users leave traces everywhere except where you would expect to find them.
>
> Read what they wrote while complaining about something else.
