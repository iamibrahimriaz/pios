# Evaluation

---

# Overview

There is one test underneath all of these: **could this have been written without
meeting anyone?** If it could, it was.

---

# Exercise 1 — The Recognition Test

Write a current workflow for someone you know does this job. Then show it to them.

**Passing looks like.** They correct a detail and add one you did not know about.

**Failing looks like.** They agree with all of it politely. Complete agreement usually
means the description was general enough to be unfalsifiable.

---

# Exercise 2 — Strip the Solution Out

Take five feature requests — from a backlog, a review site, anywhere. Rewrite each as a
job containing no product noun.

**Passing looks like.** At least two of the five, once stripped, suggest a different
solution than the one requested.

**Failing looks like.** All five jobs map back to the original feature. That means you
renamed rather than reframed.

---

# Exercise 3 — Split on Behavior

Take a market you know and propose three segments. For each pair, state one thing they
do differently that would change the product.

**Passing looks like.** You can do it for every pair, or you merge two segments.

**Failing looks like.** The differences are about size or budget only. Those are
pricing segments, which is module 06's concern, not behavioral ones.

---

# Exercise 4 — Price the Move

Pick a tool you personally use at work and calculate what it would cost you to switch
to a better one. All five components, in hours or in money.

**Passing looks like.** A number large enough to explain why you have not switched to
something you already know is better.

**Failing looks like.** A small number. If the honest total is small and you still have
not moved, one component was not priced — usually perceived risk or the effect on other
people.

---

# Exercise 5 — Interview Without a Hypothetical

Conduct a fifteen-minute conversation about a task, using only questions about the
past. No "would you," no "do you think you might."

**Passing looks like.** You learn about a workaround you did not anticipate.

**Failing looks like.** You run out of questions in six minutes. That is normal at
first and it is the skill being tested — past-tense questioning requires following what
was said rather than working through a list.

---

# Exercise 6 — Profile the Status Quo

Write the paper pad, the spreadsheet, or the memory-based process as though it were a
competitor: what it costs, what it does well, who it suits, why people stay.

**Passing looks like.** It reads like a formidable competitor, because it is one.

**Failing looks like.** A list of its weaknesses. That is advocacy, and module 05 will
inherit it as a competitor profile.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Produces personas; cannot describe a single real workflow end to end |
| **Working** | Documents behavior, but only the part inside software |
| **Competent** | Jobs stated without solutions; switching cost priced across all five components |
| **Fluent** | Finds the workaround, treats the status quo as a serious competitor, and can say which of their findings came from observation and which from inference |

The move from working to competent is usually caused by one thing: watching someone do
the task instead of asking them about it.

---

# A Note on What Cannot Be Assessed Here

Whether these users are worth serving. Nothing in this module answers that, and the
warmth of good user research makes it tempting to conclude it. A vividly described
population can be small, unreachable, unable to pay, or already well served — and
modules 04, 05 and 06 exist to find that out.

Sympathy is not a market signal. This module produces sympathy as a side effect and
should not be read as producing evidence of demand.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> The test is whether a line in your document could only have been written by someone
> who was there.
>
> Count those lines. That count is the real assessment.
