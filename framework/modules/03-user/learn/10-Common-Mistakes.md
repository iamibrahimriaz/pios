# Common Mistakes

---

# Overview

User research fails in a particular way: the output is warm, readable and plausible,
and nobody wants to be the person who challenges it. These are the failures worth
challenging.

---

# 1. The Persona Assembled from the Team's Assumptions

**What it looks like.** A named character with a photograph, a job title, three goals
and three frustrations, all of which the team already believed.

**Why it is tempting.** It is fast, it looks like research, and it produces something
everyone can agree with — because it contains nothing anyone disagreed with beforehand.

**What it costs.** It is inherited by module 04 as a description of a real population.
Problems are then ranked for a person who was invented, and module 08 traces features
to those rankings, so the whole chain is internally consistent and externally
unconnected.

**Instead.** Include at least one detail nobody expected. If the persona contains no
surprise, it contains no information.

---

# 2. Segments That Are Demographics

**What it looks like.** "Small teams, mid-market, enterprise." Or: "millennials."

**Why it is tempting.** These categories already exist and are easy to count.

**What it costs.** A segment is only useful if its members behave differently in a way
that changes the product. Splitting by size when the behavior is identical produces
three segments needing one product — and module 11 then builds three acquisition
strategies for one population.

**Instead.** Split where behavior diverges. If two groups do the same thing in the same
way, they are one segment with two labels.

---

# 3. The Job That Contains the Solution

**What it looks like.** "Needs a centralized dashboard to track performance."

**Why it is tempting.** It is what users say. They arrive with solutions because they
have been thinking about it longer than you have.

**What it costs.** Module 08's requirement-to-problem trace becomes circular. The
feature exists because the user asked for the feature — a chain that cannot be wrong
and cannot be informative.

**Instead.** Ask what they would do with it. Two or three rounds of that question
usually reaches a decision they have to make or an account they have to give, and that
is the job.

---

# 4. Asking What People Want Instead of What They Do

**What it looks like.** Survey results about desired features; interviews about
hypothetical behavior.

**Why it is tempting.** It is much easier to ask than to observe, and people answer
willingly.

**What it costs.** Stated preference is a poor predictor of behavior and an excellent
predictor of what people believe about themselves. A product built on it launches to
enthusiasm and no usage.

**Instead.** Ask about the last time. "Walk me through what happened the last time you
did this" produces behavior; "would you use a tool that…" produces politeness.

---

# 5. The Workflow Documented Only Where the Software Is

**What it looks like.** A flow that begins when the user opens an application.

**Why it is tempting.** That is the part you can see, and the part you can change.

**What it costs.** The interesting parts are usually outside: the conversation that
triggered the task, the note on paper, the message to a colleague, the wait. Module 10
then designs a flow starting from a screen, and the real starting point — a situation —
never appears in the product.

**Instead.** Start the workflow where the user's day starts touching the job, not where
your product would.

---

# 6. Switching Cost Reduced to Data Migration

**What it looks like.** "We will provide an importer."

**Why it is tempting.** It is the component that has a technical solution, so it is the
one a product team can act on.

**What it costs.** Retraining, disruption to other people, contractual timing and
perceived risk are all untouched by an importer, and together they usually exceed it.
The launch then produces agreement without movement.

**Instead.** Price all five. Three of them are addressed by sequencing and messaging
rather than by engineering, which is exactly why they get missed by engineering-led
teams.

---

# 7. The Status Quo Treated as Nothing

**What it looks like.** "Currently there is no solution; they do it manually."

**Why it is tempting.** Manual work looks like an absence rather than a competitor.

**What it costs.** Module 05 has to score "do nothing," and the status quo has real
advantages: zero cost, zero learning, complete flexibility, and total trust. A
document that treats it as nothing hands module 05 a competitor with no profile, and
it is usually the strongest one in the set.

**Instead.** Document the paper pad as carefully as you would document a funded rival.

---

# The Pattern

| Mistake | Underlying move |
| --- | --- |
| Assumed persona | Research that confirms |
| Demographic segments | Splitting on what is countable, not what differs |
| Job with a solution in it | Accepting the user's first framing |
| Asking what people want | Preferring the answerable question |
| Workflow starting at the screen | Looking only where you can act |
| Migration as switching cost | Pricing the component you can engineer |
| Status quo as nothing | Mistaking absence of product for absence of solution |

Five of the seven share one root: **looking where it is convenient to look.** That is
the characteristic failure of this module, and it produces documents that are
comfortable throughout and load-bearing nowhere.

---

> **Mistakes Principle**
>
> Nobody argues with a persona.
>
> That is precisely the problem — an artifact nobody can disagree with is inherited
> by six downstream modules as though it had been checked.
