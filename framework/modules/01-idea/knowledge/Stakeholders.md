# Stakeholders

---

# What It Is

The set of parties an idea touches, separated by the role they play rather than by their job
title.

Four roles matter, and they are often four different people:

| Role | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **User** | Who performs the work the product changes? |
| **Buyer** | Who authorizes the money? |
| **Blocker** | Who can prevent adoption without wanting anything from it? |
| **Affected** | Whose experience changes without their choosing to use it? |

Distinguishing user from buyer is a gate criterion in this module. They may be the same person —
but that must be stated, not assumed.

---

# When It Applies

In Pass 3 (Locate). The roles determine which segment `03-user` researches and which payer
`06-business` builds a model around.

---

# How to Apply It Here

**Name each role separately, even when one person fills several.** "The GP is user and buyer"
is a finding. Silence on the question is a gap that surfaces in module 06 as a business model
built for the wrong party.

**Look for the blocker specifically.** It is the role most often missed, and it is the one that
kills adoption without appearing in any research:

| Setting | Typical blocker |
| --- | --- |
| Clinical | Information governance, or the clinical safety officer |
| Enterprise | Procurement, security review, works council |
| Regulated finance | Compliance sign-off |
| Small practice | The office manager who owns the existing process |

**Name the affected parties.** In a consultation product, the patient is affected and does not
choose. Their experience is a constraint on the design and sometimes a regulatory obligation —
and they will not appear in user research unless someone puts them there.

**Ask who loses.** Every product removes something from someone: a task that justified a role, a
supplier's revenue, a manager's visibility. That party is a stakeholder even when nobody wants
them to be.

---

# Where It Misleads

**Enthusiasm from a user is not authority to buy.** A practitioner can love a product and have no
route to purchasing it. This is the most common cause of long, friendly, unclosing sales cycles,
and it is visible at this stage if the buyer is named.

**The buyer's problem is often not the user's problem.** A practice manager buys time savings; a
clinician wants to stop worrying about accuracy. A product sold on one and used for the other
disappoints both, and `06-business` cannot fix it later.

**Blockers rarely announce themselves early.** They appear after a decision has been made,
which is why naming them here — as a question if necessary — is worth more than it seems.
