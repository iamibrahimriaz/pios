# Vision

---

# What It Is

A description of the world once the product has succeeded — written in terms of the user's
situation, not the product's feature set.

Its function is **direction under uncertainty**. When later modules face a choice the research
cannot settle, the vision says which way to lean.

| Not a vision | A vision |
| --- | --- |
| "The leading platform for clinical documentation" | "A clinician finishes their last patient and goes home, with the record already complete" |
| "Best-in-class AI for healthcare" | "Nobody writes up notes after hours" |

The second column describes a changed situation. The first describes a market position, which is
an outcome rather than a direction.

---

# When It Applies

In Pass 6 (Bound), written after the problem and the scope, so that it describes the destination
of *this* idea rather than an ambition the idea was reverse-engineered to fit.

---

# How to Apply It Here

**Write it as the user's day, changed.** One or two sentences. Someone should be able to picture
it.

**Keep it beyond the MVP but attached to it.** A vision the current scope moves toward is useful.
A vision requiring a different product is a second idea, and it belongs in `scope_boundaries`.

**Use it to resolve ties, not to add scope.** A vision's job is to say which of two defensible
options is more in keeping with where this is going. Anything it justifies *building now* has
escaped its role — module 07 draws the MVP line, and it draws it from evidence.

**Keep it falsifiable in spirit.** "Nobody writes up notes after hours" could turn out to be
impossible or unwanted. "Transforming healthcare" cannot be wrong, which is why it is useless.

---

# Where It Misleads

**A vision is the most common vehicle for scope creep in the framework.** Every unargued feature
can be justified by pointing at a sufficiently large vision, and each justification is
individually reasonable. This is module 08's scope laundering, sourced from module 01.

**A grand vision can obscure a weak premise.** An idea resting on an unvalidated problem sounds
much stronger with a compelling vision attached, and the vision is easier to write than the
validation.

**Visions are usually written to be quoted.** That pressure makes them abstract, and an abstract
vision cannot resolve any actual choice — which was the only reason to have one.
