# Reflection

---

# Overview

These questions are worth asking after you have run the module on a real idea — one
you care about — rather than on a practice case. On a practice case they are merely
interesting.

---

# On the Idea Itself

**Where did it actually come from?** Not the story you would tell, the origin. A
frustration of your own, something you read, a capability you wanted to use, a
conversation. Each origin has a characteristic blind spot, and knowing which one you
are working from is more useful than pretending you started neutrally.

**What would have to be true for this to be worth doing?** Write the list before
module 02 begins. Comparing it afterward to what modules 02 through 06 actually found
is the single most instructive thing available in the framework.

**If someone handed you this idea, would you take it?** Ownership changes judgment more
than evidence does.

---

# On the Questions You Asked

**Which question did you nearly not ask?** There is usually one. Why did you nearly
skip it — was it uncomfortable, or did it seem obvious?

**Which answer surprised you?** If none did, either the idea is unusually
well-understood or the questions were confirmatory. The second is far more common.

**What did you defer?** The gate permits deferral. It does not permit forgetting.
Look at what you deferred and ask whether it was deferred because the answer is
genuinely unavailable now, or because it is unwelcome now.

---

# On the Assumptions

**Which assumption is doing the most work?** Usually one line in the brief carries the
whole premise. In a well-written brief it is tagged. In an honest one it is also
identified as the load-bearing one.

**What would change your mind about it?** If nothing would, it is not an assumption —
it is a commitment, and it should be written as one so that later modules stop trying
to validate it.

**Which of them are you emotionally attached to?** This question has no operational
answer and is worth asking anyway. Attachment is what makes a later module's
disconfirming finding get argued with rather than recorded.

---

# On the Boundary

**What did you leave in scope because cutting it felt premature?** Premature is the
word that keeps scope open. Note where you used it.

**What are you hoping nobody asks about?** Whatever it is, module 04 or module 09 will
ask, and the cost of answering rises with every module in between.

---

# On Your Own Pattern

Over several ideas, patterns emerge that a single run cannot show you:

| Look for | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| You always name the market by adjective | You are optimizing for the size of the opportunity rather than for its shape |
| You always know the user and not the buyer | You come from craft rather than from commerce, or the reverse |
| Your assumption tags cluster in one area | That area is where you are least comfortable, and it is where the run is weakest |
| Your non-goals are always safe | You are drawing the boundary where it costs nothing |

None of these are faults. They are the shape of your judgment, and the value of
noticing them is that you can staff around them.

---

# The Question Worth Returning To

> Was the idea improved by this, or only described more carefully?

Both are legitimate outcomes and they are not the same. A brief that describes the
original idea precisely has done its job. A brief that changed the idea — narrowed
it, moved it to a different buyer, found a sharper premise inside it — has done
something better, and it is worth knowing which one happened.

---

> **Reflection Principle**
>
> The idea stage is the only point in the framework where you are working almost
> entirely from your own judgment, because no evidence has arrived yet.
>
> That makes it the stage where knowing your own habits does the most good.
