# Evaluation

---

# Overview

Recognition is not skill. Everyone who reads `10-Common-Mistakes.md` agrees with it;
far fewer produce a brief that avoids all six. The exercises below are designed so
that agreeing is not enough to pass them.

This is an assessment of a person, not a gate on a run. The run's gate lives in
`core/11-Quality-Gate.md` and is checked mechanically.

---

# Exercise 1 — Reduce an Idea to One Sentence

Take any product you use daily. Write its premise in one sentence that contains no
adjective doing structural work, names a boundary, and does not describe the interface.

**Passing looks like.** A sentence someone unfamiliar with the product could use to
predict what it does not do.

**Failing looks like.** A sentence that would equally describe three competitors. If
it would, the sentence describes a category rather than a premise.

---

# Exercise 2 — Separate the Layers

Take a one-paragraph idea — your own, or one from a friend. Split every clause into
one of three columns:

| Known | Believed | Neither, and load-bearing |
| --- | --- | --- |

**Passing looks like.** The third column is not empty, and you can say what it would
take to move each item out of it.

**Failing looks like.** Everything lands in "known." That result is almost never true
at the idea stage, and producing it is the exact habit the evidence policy exists to
break.

---

# Exercise 3 — Ask the Uncomfortable Question

Write five clarifying questions about an idea you like. Then mark which one you would
least want answered.

**Passing looks like.** You can identify it immediately, and it is in the set.

**Failing looks like.** No question qualifies. That means the set was built to confirm
rather than to clarify, and the gate's "five clarifying questions" was satisfied by
count rather than by content.

---

# Exercise 4 — Name What Is Out

For the same idea, write three non-goals — things a reasonable person would expect the
product to do, that it will not.

**Passing looks like.** Each non-goal is something you would genuinely be tempted to
build, with a reason it stays out.

**Failing looks like.** Non-goals nobody would have proposed anyway. "We will not
build a social network" is not a boundary; it is a decoration.

---

# Exercise 5 — Find the Buyer

For a product you use at work, answer three questions: who chose it, who pays for it,
and who would have to agree before it could be removed.

**Passing looks like.** Three different answers, or a clear statement of why they
collapse into one.

**Failing looks like.** Not knowing. This is a useful failure — it is exactly the gap
module 06 inherits when the idea brief was silent.

---

# Rubric

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Not yet** | Can restate the six common mistakes; still produces briefs containing three of them |
| **Working** | Catches the mistakes on review, after writing |
| **Competent** | Writes with the boundary and the payer already named; tags assumptions without prompting |
| **Fluent** | Asks the uncomfortable question first, and treats a brief that is mostly assumptions as a normal starting state rather than as a problem |

Most people plateau between "working" and "competent," and the specific thing that
moves them is doing Exercise 2 on their own writing rather than someone else's.

---

# A Note on What This Cannot Assess

None of this tells you whether the idea is good. The framework offers no such test at
this stage and the absence is deliberate. An idea brief that is precise, bounded and
honestly tagged can describe a product nobody wants — and finding that out is the job
of the eleven modules that follow, not this one.

Assessing framing and assessing merit are different skills. Confusing them is how a
well-written brief acquires unearned authority.

---

> **Evaluation Principle**
>
> You have learned this stage when a brief full of tagged assumptions stops feeling
> like weak work.
>
> That reaction is the real assessment. Everything else here is a way of getting to it.
